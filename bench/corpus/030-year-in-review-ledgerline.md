A year ago this month, four of us sat down and decided to build an invoicing tool for small businesses, the kind of decision that sounds simple in a single sentence and turns out to contain a thousand smaller decisions once you actually start. This is my attempt at a proper retrospective, not the kind you write for a sprint but the kind you write when enough time has passed that you can see the shape of the whole year rather than just the week you're standing in. We started with almost nothing: a name, a rough sense that invoicing software for small businesses was underserved by tools built for enterprises with entire finance departments, and a shared conviction that four people could ship something real without needing to raise money first or hire beyond ourselves. Dana had the backend instincts, Kofi the operational discipline, Priyam the frontend craft, and Lotte the product sense to keep us honest about what actually mattered to a real customer versus what merely felt interesting to build. None of us had built something quite like this before, together or separately, and there was a real question in the first few months about whether four strong-opinioned people could actually converge on decisions fast enough to ship anything, rather than debating architecture forever in the abstract. Looking back at [[003-dana-dev-journal-kickoff]], the very first entry in this whole project's written history, it's striking how many of the early instincts held up: boring technology, Postgres over anything trendier, small deliberate scope over impressive-sounding ambition. Some of it didn't hold up at all, and that's arguably the more interesting story to tell honestly, since a retrospective that only confirms what you already believed isn't really doing its job. We shipped our first invoice in month three, our first paying customer in month nine, and as of this writing we have thirty-one organizations paying us real money every month, which still doesn't feel like a huge number until you remember it's thirty-one real businesses trusting us with something as sensitive as their money and their customer relationships, which is not a trust either given lightly or one we take lightly in return.

## What we got right

Choosing boring, well-understood technology over anything trendy meant we spent our limited time on the actual product rather than fighting infrastructure, and a year later that decision looks better with hindsight than it did at the time. Keeping the team at four and resisting early pressure to hire faster kept communication overhead low enough that we could actually agree on things quickly, which mattered more than raw output capacity in year one specifically. Writing decisions down as we made them, even small ones, turned out to matter more than expected — this vault of notes has settled more than one "wait, why did we do it that way" conversation months later, just by letting whoever asked go read the reasoning themselves. We also got the feedback loop right early, talking to users weekly, which kept us honest.

## What we got wrong

We underestimated how much operational maturity — proper environment isolation, deploy safety checks, the kind of unglamorous plumbing described in [[023-dana-launch-day-journal]] — would matter this early, treating it as a later-stage concern until a near-miss on launch day forced the issue immediately and uncomfortably, with far less warning than any of us would have liked given what was actually at stake that morning. We also spent too long deferring the row-level security question from [[002-architecture-overview]], letting a known architectural gap sit longer than it should have simply because nothing had broken yet, which is never actually a good reason to leave a real risk unaddressed for months at a stretch. Separately, we were slower than we should have been to invest in production-scale testing data, a gap Priyam's performance investigation eventually forced us to confront directly rather than continuing to assume our tiny seed datasets were representative of anything a real customer would actually do with the product day to day, across a full billing cycle rather than a quick demo click-through.

## The customers who taught us the most

Our first real customer, the landscaping business Lotte onboarded personally, taught us more about actual usage patterns in one quarter than months of our own internal testing had, most visibly through the performance issue Priyam chased down and documented in [[028-priyam-performance-tuning-journal]], which we'd never have found against our own thin seed data no matter how carefully we'd looked at it ourselves beforehand. A second customer, a small catering company, pushed us hard on recurring invoices working correctly across irregular monthly schedules, which turned out to be a genuinely harder problem than any of us had assumed going in, given how many edge cases exist around partial months, skipped weeks, and mid-cycle price changes that our first design hadn't accounted for at all. A third customer, larger than the other two and closer to what we'd call enterprise, is the one who prompted the security review in [[027-security-review-doc]] as part of their procurement process, and going through that exercise properly, rather than treating it as a box to check quickly, ended up sharpening several assumptions we'd never actually written down or tested rigorously before someone with real leverage asked us to prove them. Each of these three customers, in their own distinct way, pushed the product further than our own internal instincts alone ever would have, and it's hard now to imagine having made several of this year's most important calls without their specific, sometimes uncomfortable pressure forcing the question in the first place, rather than us simply guessing at what mattered from the comfort of our own assumptions about who we were building for.

## Where we're headed

Multi-currency support, long deferred, is next, tied to the exchange-rate question in [[002-architecture-overview]]. Row-level security is finally this quarter's top priority rather than a someday item. Growth stays steady rather than explosive, which suits four people fine, for now at least.
