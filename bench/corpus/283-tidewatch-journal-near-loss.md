# Journal — the near-loss at table three

2026-02-03

We almost lost a full playtest group tonight, and not in the good "we nearly beat the storm" sense — in the "someone put the rulebook down and didn't pick it back up" sense, which is the outcome I dread most with Tidewatch. Four new players, none of whom had touched a cooperative game before, sat down at seven for what was supposed to be a ninety-minute demo session. By the forty-minute mark two of them had gone quiet, arms crossed, watching Kofi and me argue about whether the storm surge triggers before or after the tide marker advances. That argument is exactly the kind of thing that should never happen in front of new players, because it signals the rules aren't settled even to us, and if the designers look unsure, why would a stranger trust the system enough to keep engaging with it.

The rules question was legitimate — the storm mechanic card is genuinely ambiguous about sequencing, and I've meant to fix the wording for two weeks — but the timing could not have been worse. Watching from the outside, pretending to take notes, I felt the moment the table's energy left the room. It's physical, not just a vibe; shoulders drop, eye contact stops, someone checks their phone under the table. I've felt it before during early prototypes, but never this sharply, never with people who had no prior investment in the game succeeding. They weren't here because they loved board games enough to forgive a rough edge. They were here because a friend invited them, and that friendship, not our design, was keeping four people in their seats.

## What actually happened

Once Kofi and I agreed on a temporary ruling — surge resolves before the tide marker moves, full stop, we'll fix the wording later — the table didn't snap back to life immediately. It took another round, maybe eight minutes, before anyone smiled again. What brought them back wasn't a rules clarification; it was a near-miss. The warden pawn was one storm token from being swept off the board, and Priyam's partner, who had barely spoken all session, made a clever call to sacrifice a supply card for an extra turn. That single moment did more for the table's trust than any explanation we could have given. It reminded me Tidewatch lives or dies on tension recovering into triumph, not rules clarity alone.

Afterward I asked the two quiet players directly what almost lost them, rather than assuming, because I've been wrong before when I guessed instead of asked. Both said watching the designers visibly disagree made them feel the game wasn't finished enough to be worth their evening, and it took real effort to re-engage after that. One gently suggested ambiguous rules disputes should happen after the session, written down, not debated live in front of people learning the game for the first time. It's obvious advice in hindsight and I'm slightly embarrassed it took a near-loss to internalize it as a hard rule rather than a vague intention.

I also think this is a design problem, not just a facilitation problem. If the storm surge sequencing were unambiguous in the card text, there would have been nothing to argue about in front of anyone. Kofi has said for weeks that the storm cards need a pass focused purely on sequencing clarity, independent of balance, and tonight is the clearest evidence yet that he's right and I've been deprioritizing it in favor of flashier problems like the token shortage from [[281-tidewatch-tide-token-standup]]. Clarity problems are quieter than balance problems until the exact moment they aren't, and by then you're watching real people disengage from something you've spent a year building.

## What I'm changing

Starting with the next session, any rules disagreement between facilitators gets tabled silently with a hand signal we agree on beforehand, resolved off to the side, never debated out loud at the table where new players can see designers disagree about their own game. I'm also moving the storm card sequencing rewrite ahead of the balance work on my list this week, since tonight made obvious which one actually costs us players when it goes wrong versus which one just costs us elegance. Kofi is drafting the clearer wording and I'll review it before Thursday, and we're agreeing on the hand signal beforehand so neither of us has to improvise it under pressure at a real table again, which is exactly how tonight's argument started in the first place. I'm also writing a short facilitator checklist so anyone running a demo table, not just Kofi and me, knows the same rule applies to them without ever needing to be told after their own rough session.

## Small good thing

Despite the rocky middle, three of the four players asked when they could play again, and the one who made the sacrifice play texted Priyam afterward asking to join the next open playtest slot, which felt like a genuinely earned win after such a shaky start to the evening. I keep coming back to that one decision, small as it was, as proof the core loop still works even when everything around it wobbles badly. It's easy to fixate on the near-loss and forget that a stranger, given one real choice that mattered, chose bravery over caution and the table rewarded her for it immediately, out loud, with real enthusiasm, which is the exact feeling I want every Tidewatch table to reach eventually, rough start or not.
