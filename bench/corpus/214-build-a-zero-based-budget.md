We switched to a zero-based budget in October after years of loosely tracking spending and wondering where the money went. It took two evenings to set up properly and about three months to feel natural. This is the process we used, mostly so I can hand it to Kofi next time he asks how we actually do this month to month.

## The full process

Start with last month's take-home pay, the actual number that landed in the checking account, not the gross salary and not an estimate. Every dollar of that number needs a job before the month begins, which is the entire point of zero-based budgeting: income minus allocations should equal exactly zero, not a vague surplus you hope to save "if there's anything left." List every fixed expense first, since those are the easiest to nail down and rarely change month to month. Rent or mortgage, insurance premiums, phone and internet, any loan minimums like the ones tracked in [[212-debt-payoff-plan]], and subscriptions you've actually decided to keep. Add these up and you'll usually find they eat forty to fifty percent of take-home pay for a typical household, sometimes more in an expensive city.

Next come the variable-but-necessary categories: groceries, gas or transit, utilities that fluctuate seasonally, and a reasonable household maintenance line. These need estimates rather than exact figures, and the estimate should come from your own history, not a generic percentage some article told you to use. Pull three months of bank statements and average them; that average is your starting number, even if it feels higher than you'd like. You can work on lowering it later, but budgeting against a fantasy number just guarantees you'll blow through it every single month and lose trust in the whole system within a few weeks.

Then allocate to savings and debt goals before touching discretionary spending, not after. This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that actually makes zero-based budgeting different from just tracking expenses after the fact. Decide the retirement contribution, the extra debt payment, the emergency fund top-up, and treat those like fixed expenses that get paid to your future self first. We automate all of these as transfers that happen the day after payday, so the money is gone before it has a chance to get spent on something less deliberate. Automating removes the willpower requirement entirely, which matters more than any spreadsheet trick, because willpower runs out by the third week of the month and a good system shouldn't depend on it holding up.

Only after fixed costs, variable necessities, and savings goals are allocated do you assign whatever remains to discretionary categories: dining out, entertainment, hobbies, gifts. If the remainder is smaller than you'd like, that's useful information rather than a problem to hide from — it tells you exactly where the real tension in the budget lives, whether that's income, fixed costs, or savings rate, instead of vaguely feeling broke without knowing why. We split our discretionary remainder into three sub-envelopes: dining out, "fun money" that either of us can spend with zero questions asked, and a shared miscellaneous catch-all for the stuff that doesn't fit anywhere else, like a birthday gift or a one-off home repair under $100.

The last step, and the one we skipped for the first two months and immediately regretted, is reconciling against actual spending weekly rather than waiting until month-end. A five-minute Sunday check-in catches an overspent category while there's still time to adjust the following week's plan, rather than discovering the damage after it's already done and unfixable for that cycle. We do this during the same sit-down as our [[211-budget-review-sync]], which keeps it from becoming its own separate chore that's easy to let slide when the week gets busy.

## What changed for us

The biggest shift wasn't the spreadsheet, it was the ordering: paying our future selves and our debts before deciding what's left for fun. Overspending dropped almost immediately once every dollar had an assigned job in advance. We still miss a category occasionally, especially with irregular expenses like car registration, but we catch it within a week now instead of discovering it two months later during a confused end-of-quarter reconciliation session. It also ended a recurring argument about who spent what, since the plan became the reference point instead of memory.
