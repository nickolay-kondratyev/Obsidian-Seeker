October 4. Just back from a long weekend road trip with Lotte and Tomasz to watch a regional stage race two hours north, the first time any of us have gone to watch professional racing in person rather than just following it online. Left Friday straight after work, which meant hitting traffic leaving the city and not arriving at the campsite until well after dark, fumbling to pitch tents by headtorch while arguing cheerfully about whose fault the late start actually was. Tomasz blamed Lotte for the extra stop at the service station; Lotte blamed Tomasz for forgetting the tent poles the first time and having to turn back fifteen minutes into the drive. Saturday was the mountain stage, which meant a four-mile walk up from the nearest parking to a viewing spot on the final climb, carrying a cooler between us and complaining the entire way up about carrying a cooler between us, which in hindsight was a fairly predictable outcome given none of us had thought to check how far the walk actually was before committing to a spot partway up the mountain rather than somewhere closer to the finish line itself. By the time we actually reached the spot, legs already tired from the walk alone, the caravan of sponsor vehicles was already rolling past throwing out branded keyrings and stickers, which the three of us scrambled for with far more enthusiasm than any of us expected to feel about free stickers, genuinely surprising ourselves in the process.

## Watching the Race

Nothing quite prepares you for how fast professional riders actually climb compared to what you expect from watching on a screen; the peloton came past our spot in a blur that felt more like a fast descent than an uphill effort, and it was over in what felt like seconds after two hours of waiting and roadside picnic snacks. The gap between the front group and the stragglers was enormous by the time the last riders came through, maybe twenty minutes behind the leaders, still riding hard despite clearly being out of contention, which struck all three of us as its own kind of impressive in a different way from the winners. We'd brought a small cowbell Lotte found in a charity shop specifically for the occasion, and rang it enthusiastically for every single rider that passed, including, embarrassingly, several riders we cheered for twice because the road doubled back on itself further up the mountain and we hadn't quite worked that out from the course map beforehand. Met a French couple who'd travelled considerably further than us and had genuinely useful tips about which climbs were worth watching next year, plus a retired local rider who told us stories about racing this exact mountain himself decades ago, back when the road wasn't even fully paved for the final two kilometres and riders occasionally abandoned rather than face the descent in bad weather. We stayed for the whole afternoon, ate too many snacks, and got sunburned in a way none of us had planned for despite it being October, since the mountain air was deceptively strong at that altitude even with a cool breeze making it feel noticeably milder than it actually was in the direct sun. By the time we walked back down to the car, all three of us were sunburned across the nose and slightly hoarse from shouting, and thoroughly agreed it had been entirely worth the long walk up and the late Friday drive to get there in the first place.

## What Stuck With Me

Mostly the sheer scale of effort on display, sustained over a distance and gradient that would break most of us within the first few kilometres, let alone the fourth hour of a stage. It's given me a completely different appreciation for our own hill climb event, which suddenly feels both more approachable and more respectable at once, a strange combination but an honest one. Also stuck with me: how genuinely kind and chatty the whole crowd was, strangers sharing food and stories for hours despite having nothing in common beyond standing on the same patch of mountainside. I keep thinking about the retired rider's stories too, particularly one about riding this exact climb in a storm decades ago with tubular tyres and no proper rain gear, treated almost as a funny anecdote now but clearly miserable at the time. There's something about hearing history directly from someone who lived it, rather than reading it online, that makes the sport feel more connected across generations than I'd appreciated before, us included in some small way just by standing there and cheering.

## Bringing It Home

We've half-jokingly floated the idea of a club trip to watch a stage next year, properly organised this time with an actual viewing spot chosen in advance rather than picked at random off a course map at midnight after everyone's already tired from the drive. Worth raising at a committee meeting once the website project and everything else calms down; this felt like exactly the kind of low-cost, high-enjoyment social event the club doesn't do enough of compared to organised rides and fundraisers, and it wouldn't need much beyond a shared car journey, camping gear, and people willing to walk further than strictly necessary for a good view. Tomasz has already started checking next year's race calendar for a repeat trip.
