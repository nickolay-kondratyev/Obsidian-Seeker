Evaluating whether to upgrade the 100mm macro lens, now showing real wear after three years of near-daily jewelry work, or replace it with a newer model with better close-focus performance for the increasingly specialized reflective-surface bookings that now make up a growing share of the studio's revenue. This isn't a purely reactive repair decision, the lens still technically works, it's a deliberate question about whether the current tool is holding back the level of detail clients are increasingly asking for in facet-level jewelry work specifically.

## The Current Lens's Limits

The existing macro lens hits true 1:1 magnification but starts showing softness at the very edge of the frame past about f/11, which matters more than it used to now that focus-stacked jewelry work often needs the whole frame sharp corner to corner rather than just the center where the lens has always performed well. The focus ring's mechanical click, first flagged back in [[181-monthly-gear-check-in]], has gotten slightly worse since then, not enough to affect image quality directly but enough that I no longer fully trust it during a long stacking sequence where consistent, repeatable behavior actually matters more than raw optical sharpness at any single aperture. The repair shop quoted $180 to service the click, which fixes the symptom but does nothing for the edge softness, so a repair alone wouldn't actually solve the underlying problem driving this evaluation.

## Candidate Replacements

Two realistic options after research and one in-person test at a local camera shop: a newer macro lens from the same manufacturer with improved edge sharpness and true 1:1 magnification at a slightly longer working distance, useful for lighting clearance on small reflective pieces, and a third-party macro option that tests slightly sharper in independent reviews but has noticeably slower, less reliable autofocus, which barely matters for locked-down tripod stacking work but would matter if I ever wanted the same lens to double for handheld detail work on furniture or apparel bookings, which happens occasionally and is worth weighing even though it's not the primary use case driving this whole evaluation in the first place. Priority order for the decision: edge-to-edge sharpness for stacking work first, working distance for lighting clearance second, cost third, versatility for non-macro use a distant fourth, since the primary job this lens has to do well is the jewelry and reflective-surface work that's become the studio's actual specialization and revenue driver, not general-purpose flexibility across every category of client the studio has shot for historically.

## Budget

Roughly $1,400-1,800 depending on which option, funded from the equipment budget rather than the studio relocation budget, kept deliberately separate per [[182-studio-relocation-project]]'s own budget lines so the two decisions don't quietly bleed into and distort each other's numbers this quarter. The old lens, still functional, will likely sell for $300-400 used, partially offsetting the cost.

## Decision and Next Steps

Leaning toward the same-manufacturer replacement for the working-distance advantage and native autofocus reliability, but want to rent it for one real paid jewelry shoot before committing to a purchase, rather than trusting a camera-shop demo session alone, since a single controlled demo rarely reveals how a lens actually behaves across a full working day of real client pressure and varied subject matter. Renting first costs an extra $60 but meaningfully de-risks an $1,800 decision, and I'd rather spend the extra money confirming the choice under real conditions than return a lens after the fact and lose time re-researching from scratch if the rental reveals a problem the shop demo didn't surface. Booking the rental for next week's jewelry client, who's aware I'm testing new gear and seemed genuinely fine with it once I explained the reasoning.
