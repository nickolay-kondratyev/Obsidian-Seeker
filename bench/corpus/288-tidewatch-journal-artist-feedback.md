# Journal — hard feedback on the lighthouse art

2026-01-30

I had to give Priyam some genuinely hard feedback today about the lighthouse keeper portraits, and I've been dreading this conversation for almost two weeks, which is far too long to sit on something that affects her actual working time. The four faction portraits she delivered last week are technically gorgeous — the linework alone is better than anything in our last prototype — but three of the four read as nearly identical in silhouette from across a table, which matters enormously for a cooperative game where players need to recognize each other's warden at a glance during a tense round. I ran a quick informal test with Kofi and Tomasz, showing them thumbnail-sized versions from across the room, and both mixed up two of the portraits immediately, which confirmed what I'd suspected but hadn't wanted to say out loud yet. I kept putting it off because the art is so clearly good, and I worried a structural complaint would land as dismissive of the craft, even though waiting wasn't protecting anyone, least of all Priyam.

## The conversation itself

I called Priyam rather than messaging, since this felt like the kind of feedback that reads as much harsher in text than intended, and I wanted her to hear the tone alongside the words. I opened by being specific about what's working — the linework, the mood, the way each portrait captures a distinct personality even before you consider silhouette — because leading with genuine praise before the hard part isn't flattery, it's accuracy, and skipping it would misrepresent how good most of this work actually is. Then I walked through the silhouette test results plainly, without softening the actual numbers, and asked whether she'd tested readability at table distance during her own process or only up close on a monitor, which turned out to be exactly the gap. She'd been designing at a scale and viewing distance that doesn't match how players will actually encounter these portraits during a real session, hunched over a table, checking pawns quickly mid-turn rather than admiring individual artwork. I made a point of framing this as a shared blind spot rather than her mistake alone, since none of us had flagged the viewing-distance problem earlier when we reviewed sketches together, and I should have caught it sooner given how many playtest sessions I've personally sat through watching people squint at pawns from across a table without connecting that observation to the portrait work at all. It's the kind of gap that's obvious in hindsight and completely invisible while you're heads-down reviewing thumbnails on a screen at arm's length, one at a time, rather than side by side at the scale a real table actually uses.

## Her reaction

Priyam took it better than I feared, though I could hear the disappointment in the pause before she responded. She said she'd been so focused on getting each portrait's character right that she hadn't stepped back to compare all four at a small scale, an easy trap when deep in detail work on one piece at a time. She asked for the raw comparison images and I sent them immediately rather than paraphrasing, since seeing the confusion firsthand lands differently than hearing it described secondhand.

## Where we landed

We agreed on a path forward that doesn't throw away the existing work: Priyam will adjust silhouette elements specifically — hat shape, coat outline, held object — for the two most similar portraits, while keeping the linework and coloring largely intact, since those aren't the actual problem. She's going to build a small silhouette-only comparison sheet as a working tool before touching the full portraits again, testing recognizability at reduced scale before investing more detail time into pieces that might still fail the same test. I offered to sit in on that comparison pass so she isn't making the recognizability call alone a second time, and she agreed that would help, especially since I'm the one who'll keep running informal tests like today's with whoever happens to be in the room. We also talked briefly about whether this changes the timeline for the full art pass referenced in [[277-tidewatch-component-list]], and agreed it adds maybe a week but is clearly worth it rather than shipping portraits that actively work against the gameplay they're meant to support during a fast-paced storm round. Priyam also raised the idea of adding a small colored corner marker to each portrait as a backup recognition cue, independent of silhouette, in case two adjusted portraits still read as similar under bad lighting at a convention table, and I liked this as a cheap insurance measure rather than a replacement for the silhouette fix itself. We agreed she'd prototype that marker alongside the silhouette work rather than treating it as a separate later task, since testing them together will tell us faster whether either fix alone would have been sufficient on its own. Before ending the call we set a concrete checkpoint: Priyam will share the silhouette comparison sheet by the middle of next week, and I'll run the same informal recognition test again with a fresh group of playtesters who haven't seen either version, so the comparison isn't biased by anyone already knowing which portrait is which. If the revised set still fails that blind check, we agreed to escalate to a more involved redesign rather than iterating indefinitely on small tweaks that might never fully close the gap.

## What I want to remember

Next time I have feedback like this, don't sit on it for two weeks. The delay didn't make the conversation easier, it just meant Priyam had less runway to fix it before the print deadline tightens further this spring, and I owe her an earlier heads-up next time something like this surfaces during a review.
