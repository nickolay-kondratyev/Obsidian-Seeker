# Passive Solar Considerations for a 1960s Bungalow

I got interested in passive solar design almost by accident, while trying to figure out why the kitchen's east-facing window makes the room uncomfortably warm by mid-morning most of the year, even in early spring before summer heat becomes the obvious culprit everyone assumes it must be. That question sent me down a longer rabbit hole into how much of a house's comfort, and its heating and cooling bills, comes down to decisions made at the design stage about window placement, roof overhang depth, and thermal mass, decisions our 1962 builders almost certainly weren't thinking about in the deliberate way a modern passive solar architect would, but which they sometimes got surprisingly right anyway, more by regional convention than conscious design philosophy. The basic idea of passive solar design is simple enough to state plainly: orient and shape a building so the sun does useful work in winter, warming interior spaces through south-facing glass and thermal mass that releases heat slowly after dark, while getting shaded out of the same spaces in summer by roof overhangs sized specifically for the sun's higher summer angle, without needing any mechanical system to actively manage that seasonal difference at all. It sounds almost too simple to be effective, but the physics behind it is well understood and has been for decades, even if it fell out of mainstream residential practice for a long stretch of cheap-energy decades when nobody needed to think that carefully about a window's orientation, since mechanical heating and cooling could simply paper over any design mistake at a cost nobody worried much about paying. It's worth noting too that passive solar thinking predates mechanical heating entirely; vernacular building traditions across many climates arrived at broadly similar solutions long before anyone had a name for the underlying physics, simply because houses that got the orientation wrong were noticeably less comfortable to actually live in, and that discomfort was its own slow, informal feedback loop shaping how later houses in the same region got built, generation after generation, without anyone writing the principle down explicitly anywhere.

## Our House's Mixed Record

Our own house, it turns out, has a mixed relationship with these principles depending on which side of the building you're standing in front of. The south-facing living room windows, unusually generous for the house's age, sit under a deep original eave that shades them almost completely by early June and lets in full winter sun by December, close to textbook design whether or not the builder thought about it that way or just followed regional convention. The east-facing kitchen window, by contrast, has no meaningful overhang, catches full morning sun with nothing to filter it, and heats the room before anyone's had coffee, a problem east and west exposures share more than south-facing ones, since the sun sits lower in the sky during morning and evening hours.

## What This Means for the Kitchen Redo

Knowing this changed one small but real decision in the kitchen renovation: we're adding a simple exterior shade, not full sun control but enough to cut the worst of the morning glare, rather than assuming better insulation or a smarter thermostat alone would solve a problem that's fundamentally about window orientation and unmanaged solar gain rather than anything to do with the building envelope's thermal performance in the way people usually assume these problems work by default. We looked at interior solutions first, cellular shades and the like, but Kofi pointed out that stopping the heat before it hits the glass at all works dramatically better than trying to manage it once it's already indoors radiating off every surface it touches, which is really just passive solar logic applied backward to a retrofit situation rather than new construction, and it convinced me the exterior option was worth the modestly higher cost over interior blinds alone in this particular case, even though it meant one more decision layered onto an already long list this month.

## The Workshop's South Wall

The workshop, being a new structure built from scratch rather than an inherited compromise, got to apply these principles more deliberately from the start of design. Its south wall carries most of the glazing, sized and positioned with input from the same research that sent me down this whole rabbit hole in the first place, while the north wall is nearly solid, minimizing heat loss on the side of the building that gets no useful winter sun to offset it, a straightforward asymmetry that costs nothing extra to build but meaningfully changes the space's comfort and heating load across a full year of actual use. The electrician's space heater sizing, discussed separately in the wiring plan, actually came in smaller than his first rough estimate specifically because of this glazing decision, once he factored in the free solar gain a south-facing wall like this reliably contributes through the coldest months of a typical winter here, a detail that genuinely surprised me the first time it came up in conversation with him. I hadn't expected a window placement choice to show up as a real number on an electrical load calculation, but apparently it does, in a small but genuinely measurable way that surprised both of us during that particular conversation, and it's the kind of quietly compounding detail that makes me wish I'd read up on this before finalizing the kitchen window plan too, rather than only after the fact once the workshop forced the question.

## A Caveat Worth Naming

None of this replaces proper insulation or air sealing, and I don't want to overstate what window placement alone can fix. It's a genuine, low-cost lever, but works best stacked on a tight envelope, not instead of one.
