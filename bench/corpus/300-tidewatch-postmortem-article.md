# What Two Years of Making Tidewatch Actually Taught Us

Tidewatch funded on its Kickstarter in March 2026, shipped to backers by early summer, and by any reasonable measure the project succeeded: a small, self-published cooperative board game, made by five hobbyists with day jobs, reached a few hundred households that will never meet any of us. That is not a small thing, and it did not happen the way any of us expected going in. This article is an honest accounting of what actually mattered, written a couple of months after the last box shipped, while the details are still fresh enough to be useful rather than sanded down into a tidy origin story.

The thesis, if this piece has one, is that the parts of the process we were least prepared for turned out to matter more than the parts we spent the most time worrying about. We agonized early over the storm mechanic's exact math, rebuilding the deck ratios more times than I can count, and that work mattered, but it mattered less in the end than things we barely thought about at the start: how to onboard a stranger to the rules in under five minutes at a demo table, how to phrase a Kickstarter FAQ so it didn't need constant follow-up messages, and how to pack a shipping box so it survived a courier's handling without damage. Design skill got us a good game. A completely different, less glamorous skill set got that good game into people's hands intact and understood.

None of us started this with any manufacturing or logistics experience, and it showed early, expensively, in ways this article covers honestly rather than glossing over for the sake of a cleaner narrative. What follows is organized roughly the way the project actually unfolded: what we got right in design, what surprised us in production and playtesting, what nearly went wrong before launch, and what we'd tell a version of ourselves starting over today, knowing everything we know now.

## What Worked

The storm mechanic itself, the core tension of spending actions now to prevent a worse problem later, held up remarkably well from very early prototypes all the way through to the final rulebook, needing tuning but never a fundamental rework. Running structured playtests with a consistent logging format, documented in [[274-how-to-run-tidewatch-playtest]], gave us comparable data across dozens of sessions instead of a pile of disconnected anecdotes we'd have had to reconstruct from memory weeks later. Bringing Priyam in once the core loop was stable, rather than earlier, meant her art direction never had to chase a moving target underneath it, and the art matured in step with a ruleset that had mostly stopped shifting under her.

## What Surprised Us

The single biggest surprise was how much convention demo feedback differed from home playtest feedback, not in the notes themselves but in what kind of notes each setting could even produce, a distinction covered in more depth in [[290-tidewatch-balance-article]]. Home sessions caught subtle balance issues over many rounds; convention tables caught onboarding friction in the first five minutes that our own playtesters, already fluent in the rules, could never have noticed or flagged for us, no matter how carefully we asked them to imagine being a total stranger to the game. We also underestimated how much manufacturing lead time mattered relative to design time; a printed proof correction late in the process, documented in [[293-tidewatch-journal-print-proof]], nearly slipped our launch date by weeks over a token color issue nobody had flagged as a real risk before that point. In hindsight, that single afternoon proof review probably deserved a dedicated meeting of its own far earlier in the schedule, rather than being folded quietly into an already crowded pre-launch checklist alongside a dozen other unrelated tasks competing for the same attention.

## What Nearly Went Wrong, and What We'd Do Differently

The keeper action rebalance made at the final balance meeting, barely three weeks before launch, is the clearest example of how close this project came to shipping numbers that would have undermined months of careful playtesting. Had Kofi not pulled the specific usage-frequency data showing one action was being used in seventy percent of turns, we likely would have shipped a version of Tidewatch where a single dominant strategy quietly solved most sessions, a problem invisible in casual play but exactly the kind of flaw that erodes a cooperative game's replayability over a longer ownership horizon than any pre-launch playtest window can fully capture. Separately, the fulfillment plan assumed courier volume capacity that had never actually been tested at our real scale, an assumption that happened to hold, but that we got lucky on rather than verified in advance, worth naming plainly rather than quietly taking credit for planning we didn't fully do.

Looking back, we'd start manufacturing and logistics research far earlier, in parallel with early design rather than after the game itself felt done, since lead times and vendor relationships take real calendar time that design iteration speed doesn't teach you to respect. We'd run at least one convention-style demo earlier in the process, since that setting reveals onboarding problems no home playtest group, already familiar with the rules, can fully surface on its own. And we'd build in more schedule slack around the print proof stage specifically, since it is the single point in the timeline where a real, physical problem is most likely to surface for the first time, often too late to fix without real cost to everything scheduled after it.

## Closing Thought

None of us would call ourselves manufacturing experts even now, and that's fine; the project didn't need experts, it needed five people willing to learn enough, fast enough, to ship something they were proud of putting their names on.
