Most people pack a backpack the same way every time and wonder why their hips ache by lunch. The order you load things in matters almost as much as how much you're carrying, since a badly balanced pack turns a manageable load into a miserable one within the first mile. This is the loading order I've settled on after a lot of trial and error.

## Packing Order Step by Step

Start with the sleeping bag at the very bottom of the pack, compressed into its stuff sack, since it's the item you need least often during the day and the softest thing to build a stable base on. If you're using a frameless or minimalist pack, this bottom layer is also where a foam sleeping pad can go, rolled into a cylinder that lines the inside of the pack and adds structure the pack itself lacks. Above the sleeping bag goes the bulkiest, least-dense gear you won't need until camp: a puffy jacket, camp shoes, and a stuff sack of spare clothes, all soft items that mold around whatever hard gear comes next. This middle-bottom zone is also where I put the tent body and poles if I'm not carrying them on the outside, since a tent is heavy but not something you need mid-hike. The core of the pack, sitting against your back and centered vertically, is where the heaviest items belong: food bag, bear canister if required, water reservoir, and the stove and cook set.

Weight placed here, close to your spine and at roughly shoulder height, transfers most efficiently through your hips instead of pulling your shoulders backward, which is the single biggest factor in whether a heavy pack feels manageable or brutal. A common mistake is putting the heaviest items too low or too far from your back, which creates a pendulum effect on uneven terrain and makes your hips do far more stabilizing work than they should. Around that dense core, fill the remaining space with medium-weight items: extra food, a water filter, a first aid kit, and any group gear you're carrying like a shared stove or water treatment supplies. Keep these items loosely organized by category in separate stuff sacks or dry bags, ideally color coded, so you're not unpacking half the bag to find your water filter at a creek crossing. Near the top of the main compartment, pack things you might need during a break but not constantly: a light layer, sunscreen, bug spray, and a lunch bag if you're not using a hip belt pocket for food.

The very top, whether it's a floating lid or the top of the main compartment, is for anything you might need without stopping to dig: rain jacket, map or offline GPS device, headlamp, and a snack for the next few hours. Hip belt pockets are prime real estate and should hold whatever you reach for most often without breaking stride: snacks, sunscreen, chapstick, a phone if you're navigating with it, and sometimes a small first aid item like blister tape. Side pockets are best for water bottles and trekking poles when you're not using them, and a rear shove-it pocket, if your pack has one, is ideal for a rain cover, a wet layer you just took off, or anything you want quick access to without opening the main compartment. Once everything is in, do a shakedown test: put the pack on, tighten the hip belt first, then the shoulder straps, then the load lifters, and walk around for a few minutes before you actually leave. If the pack sways side to side when you twist, something heavy is off-center or too high; if your shoulders are bearing obvious weight instead of your hips, the heavy items are probably too low or too far back.

It's worth doing this shakedown at home, not at the trailhead, because fixing a bad pack job is much easier on your living room floor than it is with a group waiting for you at the start of a trail. Small adjustments compound over a full day of hiking, so five extra minutes of careful loading in the morning consistently pays for itself by the time you reach camp with hips and shoulders that don't feel wrecked. Over several trips this loading order becomes automatic, and you'll start noticing within the first few minutes of walking if something's off, which means you can fix it at the first break instead of suffering for hours.

## After You've Packed

After a few trips you'll develop your own variations on this order based on your specific gear and how your body handles load. Some people prefer food in the very core and the bear canister slightly higher; others swap the sleeping bag and pad positions depending on pack shape. The core principle stays the same regardless: heavy and dense near your spine and mid-back, soft and light at the bottom, and quick-access items on top and in the pockets you can reach without stopping.
