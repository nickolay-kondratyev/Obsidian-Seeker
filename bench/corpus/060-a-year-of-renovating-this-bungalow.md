# A Year of Renovating This Bungalow

It has been just over a year since the contractor first walked through the kitchen with a clipboard and a flashlight, and somewhere in the last few weeks the project quietly crossed from "in progress" to "essentially done," the way these things do without ever announcing a clear finish line. The kitchen came first, gutted down to studs and subfloor and rebuilt around the zellige tile and sage cabinets we argued about for weeks in [[032-kitchen-renovation-plan]], then the workshop went up in the back corner of the lot, then the garden got redesigned around both of them once the dust had mostly settled. Along the way there were permits that took longer than promised, a roof leak that turned into its own small saga, and a budget that crept upward in the way every renovation budget apparently does regardless of how carefully you plan it beforehand. Writing this now, with the tile grout finally cured and the last of the workshop's electrical inspected and signed off, feels like the right moment to get the whole year down in one place rather than scattered across a dozen separate project notes, meeting logs, and half-finished journal entries that only make sense read in order. I keep thinking about how different the house feels to walk through now compared to a year ago, not just because the kitchen is unrecognizable but because the whole property finally feels like it has a coherent plan behind it instead of decades of accumulated, unrelated decisions layered on top of each other by whoever happened to own the place at the time. Priyam pointed out last weekend that we've started giving people the full tour unprompted whenever anyone visits, and that alone feels like a small tell about how much pride is tied up in this now. None of it happened on the original timeline, and almost none of it happened at the original budget, but standing in the kitchen this morning with actual coffee from an actual working counter, both of those facts feel like they matter a lot less than they did around month four, when every week brought a new delay and it genuinely felt like the whole thing might never finish.

## What Went Right

The sequencing turned out to be the single best decision of the year: kitchen first, since it's the room we use daily, then the workshop, which gave us staging and storage for everything after, and only then the garden, once heavy equipment stopped needing to cross the yard. Sticking with one contractor across the kitchen and most of the workshop, rather than switching for each phase to chase a better quote, paid off in continuity nobody warned us to expect, since he already understood the house's quirks by the time the harder workshop wiring came up. The permit relationship, tense at times, ended up fine once we stopped treating the inspector as an obstacle and started treating him as someone whose sign-off we needed to earn honestly.

## What We'd Do Differently

If I'm honest, we underpriced nearly everything in the first budget draft, not wildly, but consistently enough that the pattern itself was the real lesson rather than any single line item. Cabinet hardware, tile overage, and the workshop's electrical panel upgrade all came in over initial estimates, and in hindsight a flat twenty percent contingency from day one, rather than the modest ten percent we started with, would have saved several stressful budget conversations with Dana later in the year. We also should have finalized the garden's irrigation layout before the workshop foundation went in, since running conduit and irrigation lines through already-landscaped ground turned into more digging than either of us wanted, weeks we could have skipped entirely with slightly better sequencing decided up front instead of improvised as problems appeared. And I'd push harder, next time, for a written schedule with actual buffer days built in, rather than trusting verbal estimates that consistently ran two or three weeks past whatever date got mentioned in a given week's conversation.

## The Money

Final numbers, rounded: kitchen came in around thirty-eight thousand dollars against an original estimate closer to thirty-one, workshop landed near twenty-two thousand against an eighteen-thousand-dollar estimate, and the garden, done in two phases as planned back in [[037-garden-redesign-plan]], stayed closest to budget at just under thirty-four hundred dollars total. All told we're roughly twenty percent over the sum of the original three estimates, which stings less than it might have because we tracked it monthly rather than discovering the gap all at once at the very end of the year. The contingency fund, topped up twice over the year after the budget reviews with Dana, absorbed most of the overage without requiring us to touch savings meant for anything else, which in hindsight is probably the single most important financial decision in the whole project: fund the contingency properly and adjust it as real numbers come in, rather than treating the original estimate as sacred long after the ground has clearly shifted under it. Financing stayed simple throughout, a mix of savings and a modest home equity line we've been paying down steadily since spring, and we never came close to needing the second, larger line of credit we'd pre-approved just in case things went badly wrong somewhere along the way. Insurance was the one line item that genuinely surprised us: the policy needed updating twice, once after the workshop was framed and once after it was wired and occupied with real tools, and both updates bumped the annual premium more than either of us expected going in, a cost we hadn't budgeted for at all until Dana flagged it during the second review.

## One Year From Now

The only major project left on the list is resurfacing the driveway, which we've deliberately pushed to next spring rather than squeezing it into an already exhausting year, and honestly, that feels like exactly the right amount of ambition for a while.
