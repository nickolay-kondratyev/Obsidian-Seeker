# Tuning a Rear Derailleur

The most common workshop-clinic question by far. Most club members can do this themselves in ten minutes with a screwdriver once they understand what each adjustment actually does, rather than randomly turning barrel adjusters and hoping something improves, which is how most people, myself included years ago, first attempt it.

## Step by Step

Start by shifting to the smallest cog at the back and the smallest chainring at the front, giving the most chain slack to work with and the clearest view of the derailleur's resting position. Check the limit screws first, usually marked H and L on the derailleur body: the H screw sets how far the derailleur can move toward the smallest cog, and the L screw sets the largest cog limit. With the chain on the smallest cog, the upper jockey wheel should sit directly below the smallest cog, not drifting outward past it; if it does, turn the H screw a quarter turn at a time until it lines up. Shift to the largest cog and check the same alignment for the L screw, making sure the jockey wheel doesn't crash into the spokes, which is both a horrible noise and a fast way to bend a hanger or snap a chain if you're not paying attention.

With limits set, move to indexing, which is where most shifting problems actually live. Shift to the second smallest cog and turn the pedals while watching the chain. If it's slow to move up from the smallest cog, or hesitates and rattles instead of shifting cleanly, the cable has too much slack: turn the barrel adjuster counter-clockwise, a quarter turn at a time, pedalling and shifting between checks, until the chain jumps up promptly on a single click. If instead the chain overshoots and tries to climb two cogs at once, or rubs against the third cog while sitting on the second, there's too much tension: turn the barrel adjuster clockwise instead, again a quarter turn at a time rather than guessing at a bigger correction, since overcorrecting is the single most common way this process drags on far longer than it needs to.

Once the second cog shifts cleanly both up and down, work through the rest of the cassette one cog at a time, listening for any rattle or hesitation. A cassette that shifts perfectly on cogs one through five but drags on six and seven usually means the derailleur hanger is very slightly bent rather than a cable problem, since a true cable-tension issue tends to show up consistently across the whole range rather than in just one section. Checking hanger alignment needs a proper alignment tool and is worth taking to a shop rather than eyeballing it, since a hanger that's bent enough to cause shifting problems is usually bent enough that further adjustment work is just chasing a symptom rather than the actual cause.

If shifting was fine a week ago and has gradually gotten worse without an obvious cause, check cable condition before touching any adjusters at all: a fraying or corroded cable stretches unevenly under load, which no amount of barrel adjustment will properly compensate for, and you'll end up chasing the problem indefinitely as the cable continues to degrade. This is especially common through winter when road salt and grit work their way into housing that isn't fully sealed, which is most housing on most club bikes unless someone's specifically upgraded to sealed cable systems. New cables and housing are cheap relative to the frustration of an indexing problem that never quite goes away no matter how much you fiddle with the barrel adjuster on a Sunday afternoon.

Finally, once everything shifts cleanly through the full range, double check the limit screws again under actual pedalling load rather than just spinning the wheel by hand, since chain tension under real effort can reveal a limit that's set a hair too tight and causes occasional hesitation only when you're pushing hard on a climb, which is exactly when you don't want a shifting problem to show up and throw off your rhythm on the road.

## When to Give Up and See a Shop

If you've gone through the full process twice and shifting is still inconsistent, the problem is probably mechanical rather than adjustment: a bent hanger, a worn cassette, or a derailleur that's taken a knock and is no longer tracking true. There's no shame in this; even experienced club mechanics occasionally hand a bike to the local shop rather than keep chasing a problem that isn't cable tension. Better an honest ten minutes followed by a shop visit than an evening lost to guesswork.
