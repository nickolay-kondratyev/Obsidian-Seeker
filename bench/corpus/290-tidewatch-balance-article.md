# Why Cooperative Balance Isn't a Difficulty Slider

Most advice about balancing cooperative games treats difficulty as a single dial: turn it up, the game gets harder; turn it down, it gets easier. After more than a year building Tidewatch, I've come to think this framing actively misleads designers working on games like ours, where tension comes from a rising, escalating threat rather than a static opponent. A single dial assumes difficulty is one-dimensional, but what actually determines whether a Tidewatch session feels satisfying isn't how often the group wins or loses — it's whether losses feel earned and wins feel narrow. We've had sessions where a group won comfortably and left flat, and sessions where a group lost badly and left energized, already planning their next attempt before they'd finished packing up components. If difficulty alone explained satisfaction, those outcomes should have swapped, not matched the opposite of what a simple win-rate model would predict.

The real lever, at least for Tidewatch, is variance in how close each loss and each win actually is. A group that loses because the storm surge wipes out three tiles in the final round, after fighting back from a near-disaster twenty minutes earlier, remembers that session vividly regardless of the outcome printed on the scoresheet. A group that loses steadily from turn one with no recovery arc, or wins so comfortably no decision felt load-bearing, remembers neither with any fondness, because nothing in the session asked anything meaningful of them. This is why Kofi's playtest logs track more than win and loss; they track the turn a session's outcome effectively became inevitable, since a "loss" already decided by turn four is a different, worse experience than one decided on the final round after real back-and-forth tension.

Designing for variance instead of a difficulty number changes how you tune a storm mechanic. Instead of asking "should the storm hit harder," the better question is "how do we keep the outcome uncertain until as late as possible, for as many groups and play styles as we can account for." Those are different design problems, and confusing them is the single most common mistake a first-time cooperative designer makes, myself included before I understood the distinction well enough to design around it.

## The Trap of Symmetric Difficulty

Early Tidewatch prototypes used a fixed storm escalation curve: surge intensity increased by exactly one step every third round, regardless of what the players had done. This felt fair in the abstract sense that every group faced an identical curve, but it produced flat sessions almost universally, because skilled groups learned to solve the curve itself rather than respond to what was actually happening on the board, turning what should have been a tense improvised response into a rote memorized sequence executed the same way every time regardless of table composition. Weaker groups simply lost to the same curve slightly earlier, with no sense that their specific choices had shaped how the loss unfolded, close to the worst possible outcome for the memorable variance we were chasing.

## Reactive Escalation

We replaced the fixed curve with one that responds to player choices: storm intensity now escalates faster when players cluster in the safest zones and slower when they spread into exposed positions taking on real risk, which rewards exactly the kind of tension-seeking play that makes for the memorable sessions Kofi's logs are tracking. This alone pushed the average "outcome decided by" turn significantly later across our last two rounds of playtesting, and more importantly, it pushed it later inconsistently — some groups still lock in outcomes early through no fault of the mechanic, and that inconsistency is itself useful information about which specific player behaviors correlate with flat, low-tension sessions worth investigating further. It also changed how playtesters describe their own sessions afterward, unprompted, which we didn't anticipate: groups now talk about the storm reacting to them specifically, as though it were an opponent watching their choices, rather than describing it as a fixed hazard they simply had to survive regardless of what they did.

## What This Means for Playtesting

If variance in outcome-timing is the real target, then playtest data needs a different shape than a simple win/loss tally. We now track three numbers per session: final outcome, the turn the outcome effectively became inevitable, and a subjective one-to-five tension rating each player gives immediately after the session ends, all discussed in the format described in [[274-how-to-run-tidewatch-playtest]]. None of these numbers alone tells the full story, but together they let us distinguish a "hard" session from a "tense" one, which turn out to correlate far less than intuition suggests, and knowing which one we're actually looking at changes what fix, if any, a given session even needs.

This has already changed how we react to session write-ups. A session Kofi logs as a loss no longer automatically triggers a "make it easier" conversation, and a comfortable win no longer automatically triggers a "make it harder" one, because either verdict without the tension-timing context is essentially a guess dressed up as data. We've started asking a fourth, more informal question too: would this group play again tonight if we asked. That answer, more than any of the three numbers, tends to predict whether a session's design lessons are worth acting on quickly or worth waiting to see repeated across a few more groups before we touch anything mechanical based on a single data point that might just be one unusual table having an unusually good or bad night for reasons that have nothing to do with the storm mechanic itself.

## The Uncomfortable Tradeoff

Designing for variance instead of a clean difficulty curve means accepting that some sessions will feel unfair in the moment, and that's a real cost worth naming honestly rather than glossing over in an article like this one.
