# Who gets left out when frequency becomes the metric

Frequent-network redesigns have become the dominant fix-it move in transit planning over the past decade, and for good reason: ridership studies consistently show that headway matters more to riders than coverage once a route drops below roughly a fifteen-minute wait. Agencies facing flat or shrinking budgets have taken this lesson seriously, consolidating sprawling low-ridership coverage routes into fewer, denser, more frequent corridors. The math is seductive. Concentrate service on the streets that already carry the most riders, and you can promise nearly everyone a bus within fifteen minutes, at roughly the same operating cost as the old spread-thin network. Several mid-size systems that adopted this approach over the past five years reported real ridership gains within eighteen months, and consultants now pitch "frequent network first" redesigns as close to a free lunch: better service, same budget, happier riders on average.

The trouble is the phrase "on average." A redesign that raises average frequency while consolidating routes onto fewer streets necessarily produces losers as well as winners, and the losers are disproportionately concentrated among riders who were already worst served: people in low-density outer neighborhoods, older adults and people with mobility limitations for whom an extra six-minute walk to a consolidated stop is a meaningfully different trip than for an able-bodied commuter, and shift workers whose travel times fall outside the peak windows that frequency improvements usually target first. A route that ran every thirty minutes but stopped at your corner is not obviously worse than a route that runs every ten minutes but requires a half-mile walk to reach, yet redesign evaluations built around network-average wait time treat the second scenario as a straightforward win. This essay argues that transit equity analysis needs to move past average-wait-time as its primary yardstick, because that metric structurally hides exactly the tradeoffs a redesign is making, and that the people bearing the cost of those tradeoffs are rarely the people writing the evaluation.

None of this is an argument against frequent networks as a strategy. It's an argument that "frequent for most" and "equitable" are not the same claim, and treating them as interchangeable lets real, measurable harms travel under a banner of overall improvement.

## What the aggregate numbers hide

Average wait time across a redesigned network is a population-weighted statistic, and population weighting is precisely the mechanism that erases minority experience. A corridor with ten thousand daily riders whose wait falls from twelve minutes to seven pulls the average down further than a coverage route with three hundred riders whose wait rises from twenty minutes to forty pulls it up. Report the network average and the story is unambiguous good news; report the distribution and a different, messier story appears, one where a small number of riders absorbed a very large loss so that a much larger number of riders could get a modest gain. This is not a hypothetical distinction — it is what determines whether a redesign reads as an equity win or a tradeoff, and most agency dashboards are not built to show the second version.

## The people who show up in the case studies anyway

Adeyemi's fieldwork in three mid-size US systems found the predictable pattern: riders who lost service tended to be older, more likely to report a disability affecting mobility, and more likely to be traveling for a medical appointment rather than a commute than the network as a whole. Interviews conducted six months after each redesign found that a meaningful share of these riders had simply stopped making the trip rather than switching to the new, more frequent corridor a half-mile away — the walk itself was the barrier, not the schedule. None of this shows up in ridership-weighted before/after comparisons, because by definition the riders who left the system after losing their route stop appearing in the ridership counts at all. The redesign looks successful partly because the people it failed have already been counted out of the sample by the time anyone runs the after-study, which is a kind of survivorship bias specific to transit evaluation that rarely gets named as such in agency reporting.

## The counterargument, and where it holds

The strongest response from redesign advocates is that coverage-first networks fail almost everyone a little, while frequency-first networks fail a few people a lot but serve most people well, and that the second failure mode is more correctable — you can add a paratransit supplement or a shuttle for the specific corridors that lost fixed-route service, which is cheaper and more targeted than maintaining low-ridership routes system-wide indefinitely. This is a real point, and Voss makes it carefully across several case studies: targeted mitigation, done well, can outperform diffuse mediocrity, and there is no principled reason equity requires every corridor to keep identical service forever. The catch is that "done well" is doing enormous work in that sentence, and it is exactly the part of the plan that tends to get quietly dropped. In practice, mitigation funding is frequently the first thing cut when budgets tighten again a year or two later, while the frequent corridors it was meant to offset remain permanently reconfigured with no path back to the old coverage pattern. The mitigation service itself is also often demand-response paratransit with its own well-documented reliability and booking-lead-time problems — same-day trips are rarely possible, and a next-day medical appointment often requires booking a full day in advance — which makes it a poor substitute for a fixed-route stop at your corner that simply shows up on a schedule you can plan around without a phone call.

## Where this leaves an evaluation framework

Redesign evaluations should report the distribution of wait-time change by rider group, not just the network average, and should track what happens to riders who lost fixed-route access. Equity, here, means visible losers, not hidden ones.
