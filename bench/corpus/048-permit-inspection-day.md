Inspection day, finally, after weeks of the framing sitting there fully built but technically untouchable until the county sends someone out to sign off on it. The inspector arrived almost exactly on time, which Kofi said is rarer than you'd think and probably a good sign about how the rest of the visit would go. He was younger than I expected, maybe younger than me, and worked through the kitchen framing methodically with a flashlight and a small notepad, checking the header beam against the engineering letter we'd submitted weeks ago, tapping along stud spacing, and pausing for a noticeably long time at the spot where the new electrical rough-in meets the old original wiring still running through part of the wall we didn't touch. I stood in the doorway trying not to hover, which is apparently a universal instinct among homeowners during inspections, since the inspector made a small joke about it being fine to watch, most people do, he just needed us not to answer questions on the electrician's behalf if he had any.

## What He Flagged

Two things came up, neither catastrophic but both requiring action before the follow-up inspection can be scheduled and the framing can move forward toward drywall. First, one of the new outlet boxes near the window is set slightly too deep relative to where the drywall will eventually sit, which needs a spacer ring added before it'll pass, a five-minute fix according to the electrician but one that has to happen before reinspection regardless of how minor it actually is in practice. Second, and more involved, the header beam's bearing point on the exterior wall side needs an additional jack stud that wasn't quite clear from the engineering letter's language, something the inspector said comes up often enough with letters versus full stamped drawings that he wasn't at all surprised, just methodical about flagging it precisely rather than waving it through on the assumption it was probably fine. Kofi took photos of both issues immediately and said the jack stud addition is genuinely a same-day fix once the crew is back on site, nothing structural to worry about, just a documentation gap between what the engineer wrote and what the inspector needed to see confirmed in the actual framing. He also flagged, unprompted, that he'd rather over-document minor fixes like this from now on, since it saves everyone a round trip later, and he's going to start photographing bearing points before closing anything up on future jobs regardless of what the letter technically requires.

## How It Felt

Mostly fine, honestly, though there was a stretch of maybe ten minutes during the header beam discussion where my stomach dropped in a way that felt disproportionate to what turned out to be a fairly minor correction. I've noticed this pattern in myself throughout the whole renovation: any unfamiliar term from someone with more expertise than me lands harder than it should, before the actual explanation catches up and the panic deflates as quickly as it arrived.

## Talking It Through With Kofi

After the inspector left, Kofi and I sat on the back step for a while going over what had just happened, partly to make sure I actually understood the two issues correctly and partly, I think, because he could tell I needed a minute before jumping straight back into logistics mode. He explained that failed rough-in inspections on a first pass are genuinely common, closer to the rule than the exception on renovation work specifically, since new work meeting old, undocumented construction almost always throws up at least one thing an inspector wants clarified or corrected, unlike new-build work where everything is going in against a clean, fully known set of drawings from day one. That reframing helped more than I expected it to; I'd been carrying around an unexamined assumption that a flagged inspection meant we'd done something wrong, when apparently it mostly just means the system is working exactly as designed, catching small gaps before they get buried behind drywall where they'd be far more expensive and disruptive to fix later. He also walked me through the reinspection process, which just requires a phone call once the two fixes are done, no new paperwork or fee involved, and typically gets scheduled within three or four business days rather than requiring another multi-week wait in a queue. We ended up talking for almost forty-five minutes total, well past what either issue actually required, drifting into stories from his other current jobs, a bathroom remodel two streets over that's apparently had its own share of surprises behind seventies-era tile work, which made our cast iron drain and jack stud feel almost tame by comparison, and honestly a little bit funny in hindsight once the immediate stomach-drop of the morning had fully worn off. Kofi has a genuine talent for making a stressful morning feel, by the end of it, like just another normal Tuesday in this line of work, and I found myself grateful for that more than I expected to be walking into today.

## Tomorrow

Electrician comes back for the outlet spacer, Kofi's crew adds the jack stud, and we call for reinspection by end of day if both fixes go as smoothly as everyone currently expects them to, fingers firmly crossed on my end at least. I'm also going to write both fixes down properly here so future-me remembers what actually happened instead of just the stomach-drop.
