# How to Facilitate a Blind Playtest

A blind playtest is one where the facilitator gives players the rulebook and components and says almost nothing, watching them learn Tidewatch the way a stranger buying it off a shelf eventually will, without Dana or Kofi in the room to smooth confusion. It's the single best tool for catching rulebook problems before print files are locked and mistakes become expensive to fix.

## Running the Session

Set up the table exactly as a new owner would find it after opening the box for the first time: board unfolded, decks shuffled and boxed by type, tokens sorted into their trays, rulebook on top, no extra reference sheets you happen to have lying around from previous sessions. Resist the urge to tidy anything mid-session that a real unboxing wouldn't include, because the whole point of a blind playtest is catching the gap between what we assume players will figure out and what they actually do figure out, unaided, under time pressure, with no one nearby to bail them out the moment things get confusing.

Introduce the session in under two minutes. Tell players the game's name, roughly how long it takes, and that you're there to observe, not to teach — then say nothing further about rules. This is the hardest instruction to follow, especially if you're Dana or Kofi and you've watched a dozen groups make the same wrong turn on the storm mechanic. Bite your tongue anyway. If you jump in early, you learn nothing about whether the rulebook itself is doing its job in [[289-how-to-write-rulebook-faq]], and a blind playtest that gets rescued in the first ten minutes has already failed at its one purpose.

Sit somewhere you can see the table without hovering directly over anyone's shoulder — across the room if the space allows it, or at a slight angle back from the table if it doesn't. Bring a notebook or a laptop and write down timestamps for anything notable: a rules question someone asks aloud, a card someone clearly misreads, a moment where two players start arguing about what a symbol means, a long silence where nobody is sure whose turn it is. Don't write down your own opinions yet, just what happened and roughly when — you'll have plenty of time afterward to interpret it, and interpreting while still observing tends to make you see confirmation of whatever theory you walked in with rather than what's actually unfolding at the table in front of you.

Only intervene under two conditions: players are about to make a move that would corrupt the game state in a way that can't be undone and would ruin the rest of the session for everyone at the table, or fifteen minutes have passed with the group visibly stuck and unable to find a path forward on their own, checking the rulebook repeatedly without resolution. Outside those two cases, let mistakes happen and let players correct themselves or not. A group that plays an entire round with a token miscounted, then catches it themselves and fixes it, has just taught you more about the rulebook's self-correcting clarity than almost any explicit rules question could, because they found the answer without you, using only what's in the box.

When you do intervene, answer only the specific question asked, in the fewest words that resolve it, then step back again immediately rather than staying engaged in the conversation. Don't explain the reasoning behind a rule unless someone explicitly asks for the reasoning, and don't preemptively warn the table about a mistake you can see coming three turns ahead, even though every instinct will tell you to. The goal isn't a smooth session; a smooth session with heavy facilitator involvement tells you almost nothing useful about whether the game holds up once we're not in the room, which is the entire condition Tidewatch has to survive once it ships to actual customers who bought it sight unseen.

After the game ends, whether the group won or lost to the rising tide, resist debriefing immediately. Let them talk among themselves first, unprompted, for a few minutes — genuine reactions surface before you've primed anyone with questions, and priming is easy to do by accident even with a neutral tone. Once that initial energy settles, move into structured debrief questions, which are covered separately, but the short version is: ask what confused them, ask what they'd explain differently to a friend, and ask where in the rulebook they went looking for an answer versus where the answer actually was, since mismatches there point directly at what needs reorganizing before the next print pass.

## After the Session

Write up your timestamped notes into a short summary the same day, while the specific confusions are still fresh, and flag anything that recurred across more than one blind session as a likely rulebook or card-wording issue rather than a one-off fluke. Share the summary with Kofi before the next rules revision pass so it actually shapes the document instead of sitting unread. Over time these summaries build a record of which sections cause repeated trouble.
