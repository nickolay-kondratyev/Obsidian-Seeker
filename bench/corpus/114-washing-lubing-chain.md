# Washing and Lubing Your Chain

The single most impactful piece of regular maintenance for shifting quality, drivetrain lifespan, and just how pleasant a bike feels to ride, yet it's the task most new members skip entirely, until something starts grinding or skipping under load on a climb they'd rather not think about while it's happening.

## Step by Step

Start by shifting to the smallest chainring and smallest cog, which relaxes the derailleur spring tension and makes the chain easier to work with throughout the whole process. If the chain is heavily soiled, a dedicated chain cleaning tool clipped around the chain and filled with degreaser makes quick work of it, spinning the pedals backward for a minute or two until the degreaser runs mostly clear rather than the thick grey sludge that comes out on the first pass. Without a dedicated tool, an old toothbrush and a rag work almost as well, just slower and considerably messier if you're not careful about where you're working and what's underneath the bike while you do it.

Once the chain is visibly clean, rinse away any remaining degreaser, since leftover degreaser will strip fresh lubricant just as effectively as it stripped the old grime, defeating the purpose of applying new lube afterward. A damp rag run along the chain while backpedalling works fine for this; you don't need to hose the whole drivetrain down, and in fact spraying water directly at the bottom bracket or hub bearings is asking for trouble, since water finds its way past seals more easily than most people expect and can wash out bearing grease over time.

Dry the chain thoroughly before applying anything, ideally with a clean rag rather than letting it air dry, since any remaining moisture will trap dirt in the fresh lubricant and undo most of the benefit of cleaning it in the first place. This is also a good moment to inspect the chain itself: look for stiff links that don't flex smoothly, visible rust, or a chain that's clearly stretched beyond a safe wear limit, any of which mean the chain needs replacing rather than just cleaning, no matter how thoroughly you scrub it.

For lubricant, apply one drop per link directly onto the rollers, the inner part of the chain that actually contacts the cassette and chainrings, rather than spraying broadly across the whole chain and hoping for the best. Backpedal slowly while applying so each drop lands where it's needed rather than clumping on just a few links while others go dry. Wet lube works better in wet weather and lasts longer between applications, but attracts more dirt in dry conditions; dry lube is cleaner in dry weather but needs reapplying more often and washes off almost immediately in rain. Most club members switch between the two seasonally rather than committing to one type year-round, which is a reasonable approach once you've ridden enough to notice which conditions you're actually dealing with most weeks.

After applying lube, let it sit for a few minutes so it can properly penetrate into the rollers and pins rather than just sitting on the surface, then wipe the outside of the chain firmly with a clean rag. This last step matters more than people expect: excess lube on the outside of the chain does nothing useful for performance and just attracts dirt, grit, and grime, which then gets ground into the drivetrain the moment you start riding and shifting under load. A properly lubed chain should look almost dry to the touch, with the lubrication doing its work invisibly inside the chain rather than sitting visibly on the outside where it just becomes a magnet for road grit and dust picked up on every single ride.

Do this every two to three hundred kilometres in dry conditions, more often in wet weather or after riding through mud or standing water, and always after washing the bike itself, since washing strips protective lubricant along with the dirt regardless of how careful you try to be about avoiding the drivetrain directly with the hose or bucket.

## What You'll Need

Degreaser, a rag or two you don't mind ruining, a chain cleaning tool if you want to invest in one, and a bottle of quality chain lube, wet or dry depending on the season. A small drip tray or cardboard underneath saves the garage floor from stains that are surprisingly stubborn to clean once degreaser has soaked into concrete. Total cost is minimal compared to the drivetrain wear it prevents over a season of regular riding.
