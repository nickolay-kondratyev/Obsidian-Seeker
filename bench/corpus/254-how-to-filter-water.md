Water treatment on a multi-day trip is one of those things everyone has an opinion about and most people get slightly wrong in one direction or another. This is the system I've settled on after a few bad experiences, including a stomach bug from a creek I trusted too casually a few years back.

## A Reliable Water Treatment System

Start by choosing your primary method before you ever get to the trailhead: a squeeze or pump filter, a chemical treatment like drops or tablets, or a UV pen, each with real tradeoffs worth understanding rather than just copying whatever the last person on a gear forum recommended. Squeeze filters are my default for most trips: fast, no waiting, and they remove protozoa and bacteria effectively, though they don't touch viruses, which is rarely a concern in North American backcountry water but matters more in some international settings. Pump filters are heavier and slower but handle silty or algae-choked water better than a squeeze filter, which can clog fast in genuinely dirty sources, so they're worth considering if your route crosses a lot of stagnant or sediment-heavy water. Chemical treatment, whether drops or tablets, is my backup of choice, lightweight and effectively immune to clogging, though it takes real time, often thirty minutes to four hours depending on water temperature and the specific chemistry, so it doesn't work well if you're desperately thirsty right now. UV pens are fast and light but need batteries, don't work well in cloudy water since particles can shield pathogens from the light, and I've personally had one fail on a trip from a cracked casing after being jostled around in a pack, which soured me on them as a sole method.

My actual system: a squeeze filter as primary, carried in an easily accessible side pocket, and chemical drops as backup in case the filter clogs badly or fails outright, which has happened to people I hike with more than once. At each water source, look for moving water over standing water when you have the choice, since flowing water is generally cleaner and less likely to harbor concentrated bacteria than a stagnant pool, though this isn't a substitute for treatment, just a preference when sources are equally convenient. Avoid collecting directly downstream of any livestock, campsites, or obvious animal trails if you can help it, and try to collect from moving current rather than the edge of a pool, where sediment and debris tend to concentrate more heavily. Fill your dirty water bag or bottle, then filter into your clean container, keeping the two visually distinct so you never accidentally drink untreated water by mistake, which is a more common error than people expect when you're tired and moving on autopilot at the end of a long day. If you're using a squeeze filter, backflush it regularly according to the manufacturer's instructions, usually every few uses, since flow rate degrades quickly in silty water and a clogged filter at camp after dark is a miserable problem to discover.

In freezing conditions, keep your filter from freezing overnight, since ice crystals can rupture the internal membrane and destroy its effectiveness without any obvious external sign of damage, which means you could be drinking untreated water without realizing your filter has failed. Sleeping with the filter in the bottom of your sleeping bag, wrapped in a spare sock, works well in cold weather, uncomfortable as it sounds, and it's saved more than one filter on shoulder-season trips where overnight temperatures dropped well below freezing. For groups, designate one person per water stop to manage collection and treatment rather than everyone filtering individually, which saves time at breaks and means only one filter needs to be accessible rather than everyone digging through their pack. Always carry a backup method even if it's just a few purification tablets tucked in a first aid kit, since a failed primary filter with no backup on a multi-day trip turns a minor inconvenience into a genuine problem, especially far from any resupply point. Finally, treat your instincts seriously: if a water source looks or smells wrong, algae bloom, dead animal nearby, unusual color, skip it if there's any reasonable alternative, since no filter or chemical treatment is designed to handle every possible contaminant a bad source might contain.

## Keeping It Simple

None of this needs to be complicated once you've done it a few times; it becomes as automatic as setting up your tent. The main thing is having a real backup, since filters do fail eventually, usually at the worst possible moment on the trip. See [[264-how-to-set-up-offline-maps]] for the other essential system worth dialing in before a trip: navigation when you can't rely on cell signal. Between a reliable water system and reliable navigation, most of the genuine risk on a multi-day trip gets managed before you even leave the trailhead.
