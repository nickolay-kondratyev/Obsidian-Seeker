Two years in, and I finally sat down tonight to actually look back instead of just moving to the next shoot. I opened the folder of invoices from the first year next to this year's and the difference is almost embarrassing. Year one, I billed just under $19,000 total, most of it from two clients who I now realize were paying me far less than the work was worth because I had no idea what to charge. Year two closed out at just over $61,000, which still isn't a lot compared to what a studio with a full second shooter would bring in, but it's the first year where I didn't feel like I was one slow month away from needing a second job. I think what changed wasn't really the photography, which was already decent by the end of year one. What changed was that I stopped treating every inquiry like it might be my last chance and started saying no to bad-fit work. There was a client early in year one who wanted forty product shots for $300 total and I took it because I was scared of an empty calendar. I would not take that job now, and it took most of year one and half of year two to build up the confidence to actually believe that. Priyam pointed out a few months ago that my day rate had basically tripled since we started working together, and hearing it said out loud like that made it feel real in a way that just looking at the spreadsheet never quite did.

## Gear and Money

The gear side of things has stabilized more than I expected. I used to think I needed a new lens or light every few months to stay competitive, and I spent a genuinely stupid amount of money in year one on things I barely use now, like the ring light that's been in a drawer since March of last year. Somewhere around month fourteen I stopped buying gear reactively and started asking whether a purchase would actually change what I could deliver to a client, or whether it was just going to make me feel more like a "real" photographer for a week before the feeling wore off. The macro lens was the last big purchase that actually passed that test, back when the jewelry clients started becoming a real chunk of the business rather than the odd one-off. Everything since has been small stuff: batteries, a better tripod head, the softbox Dana found a rental deal on. I think the studio relocation this spring, see [[182-studio-relocation-project]], is going to be the next real gear-adjacent decision, because the taller ceilings open up shot types I've had to turn down before, like full-body apparel and larger furniture pieces shot straight-on rather than at an angle to hide the low ceiling. I'm nervous about the rent increase but less nervous than I would have been a year ago, which itself feels like progress, and I've already run the numbers three different ways to make sure the math holds even in a slower month. The client base finally has enough repeat business, roughly forty percent of this year's revenue came from clients who also booked in year one, that a slow month doesn't feel like the end of everything the way it used to. I still check the calendar first thing most mornings out of habit more than anxiety at this point, and that's a real change even if it sounds small written down like this. Bookkeeping used to be the thing I'd avoid for weeks, and now it's a Sunday-evening habit that takes maybe twenty minutes instead of a dreaded quarterly scramble through a shoebox of receipts.

## Looking at Year Three

What I want year three to look like is less about growth in raw numbers and more about protecting the parts of the work I actually enjoy, which is mostly the lighting problem-solving on tricky products like glassware, rather than the volume catalog work that pays fine but teaches me nothing new. I've been quietly saying no to more high-volume, low-complexity catalog jobs even when the day rate is decent, because they crowd out time I'd rather spend on harder bookings. Financially that might mean revenue growth slows next year, and I'm trying to make peace with that trade-off now rather than panicking in Q3. If year one was about survival and year two was about figuring out what the business actually is, I want year three to be about being deliberate, choosing the work instead of just accepting whatever comes in. That probably means raising the day rate again in spring, once the new studio is running, rather than waiting until I feel fully confident, because waiting for full confidence has never once arrived on its own.

## Not Doing It Alone

One thing I keep coming back to is how much of this depended on saying things out loud to other people instead of just guessing alone. Dana referring the contractor, Priyam noticing the day-rate trend, even a blunt comment from a client about my invoice being too cheap in year one, none of that would have landed if I'd stayed heads-down and isolated the way I was for most of that first year. Going into year three I want to keep that habit deliberately rather than letting it happen by accident, maybe by actually joining one of the local small-business groups I keep meaning to check out instead of telling myself I don't have time.
