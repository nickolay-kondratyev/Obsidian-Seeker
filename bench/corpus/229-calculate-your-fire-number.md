# How to calculate your FIRE number

A quick walkthrough for turning your spending into a target portfolio size — the number people mean when they say "FIRE number" at meetups like ours.

## Step 1: Get your true annual spending

Pull twelve months of actual spending from your budgeting tool, not a guess. If you use [[214-build-a-zero-based-budget]], the yearly total is already sitting there waiting, and you can skip most of the manual tallying other guides assume you'll do by hand.

## Step 2: Strip out anything temporary

Remove one-off expenses — a wedding, a roof repair — for a steady number.


## Step 3: Pick a withdrawal rate

Most people start with 4%, though anyone retiring decades early should read up on sequence-of-returns risk first, and consider a lower figure if it worries you.

> [!warning] The 4% rule wasn't built for 40-year retirements
> It was modeled on 30-year windows. Retiring in your 30s or 40s means a much longer runway, and many in the FIRE community use 3-3.5% instead for extra margin.

## Step 4: Do the math

Divide annual spending by your withdrawal rate. $48,000 spending at 3.5% gives a target of roughly $1,371,000 — divide by the decimal, 0.035, not the percent.

## Step 5: Sanity-check and revisit yearly

Compare the target to your current trajectory. If your contributions and expected returns don't get you there by your target year, the number or the date needs to move. Rerun this whole calculation every year, ideally around [[216-annual-financial-planning-meeting]] so it isn't forgotten between years.

## Common mistakes

Using gross income instead of spending, forgetting withdrawal taxes, and ignoring healthcare before public coverage.

## A worked example

Here's how Priyam calculated hers two years ago, roughly. Her steady-state spending after stripping one-off costs was $42,000 a year. She chose a 3.25% withdrawal rate given her long horizon and comfort with flexibility in a downturn. That put her target at about $1,292,000 — 42,000 divided by 0.0325. She rounded up to $1.35 million for a buffer, reasoning slack up front beats a stressful recalculation five years in. She hit that number last quarter, four years after first running this calculation.
