Something shifted tonight while working through more rustlings exercises after the [[066-systems-meetup-notes]] meetup last week. I've been reading about ownership for a month now, understood the rules in the abstract, could recite them if quizzed, but tonight was the first time it actually felt intuitive rather than memorized, the first time I reached for the right pattern without consciously running through the rulebook in my head first. The exercise was a small graph traversal, walking a tree of nodes and collecting values into a result vector, and my first instinct, the old instinct from years of Python and JavaScript, was to just pass references everywhere and mutate a shared collection as I went, the way I'd unconsciously reach for in almost any other language without a second thought. The compiler pushed back immediately, and instead of the usual frustration I felt something closer to recognition: oh, I see what it wants, and I actually agreed with it this time instead of just working around the error to make it compile. It's a small distinction but it felt significant sitting at the kitchen table tonight, the difference between arguing with a tool and actually collaborating with it toward the same goal, which is not a sentence I expected to write about a compiler error message a month ago when I started this whole project. I sat with that feeling for a minute before touching the keyboard again, half suspicious it was just a fluke from being tired, but it held up through the rest of the exercise too.

## Restructuring the traversal

I restructured the function to return an owned `Vec` from each recursive call and let the caller extend its own vector with the results, rather than trying to share one mutable vector across the whole recursion and fighting the borrow checker at every call site along the way. It felt less clever than the mutable-shared-state version I originally reached for, more like plumbing, moving ownership up the call stack step by step instead of threading a mutable reference through everything, but it also felt obviously correct in a way the other approach never quite did even when I finally forced it to compile. There's no aliasing to worry about, no lifetime to argue with the compiler about, just values flowing upward through return statements the way water flows downhill, following the path of least resistance rather than being pushed uphill against the type system's grain the entire time. I compared it afterward against the version I would have written in Python, where I'd have just appended to a shared list passed by reference through every recursive call without a second thought, and realized that version hides a subtle bug risk I've genuinely shipped before: two branches of the same traversal quietly stepping on each other's results under specific timing, a class of bug that's essentially structurally impossible to write with the owned-return approach the compiler pushed me toward tonight, which is a comforting thought to end the evening on. I also timed both approaches out of curiosity, not because performance matters at all for a rustlings exercise, but because I wanted to know whether the "safer" version came with a real cost attached. The owned-return version was actually marginally faster in my quick unscientific benchmark, probably because it avoids the runtime borrow-checking overhead of `RefCell` that my first attempt at working around the compiler had reached for before I gave up and did it the idiomatic way instead. That surprised me a little; I'd assumed safety and performance would trade off against each other here the way they often do in other languages I've used, and it was satisfying to find a case where they didn't.

## Why this particular exercise mattered

I think what made it click was the shape of the problem itself. Graph and tree traversals are exactly where shared mutable state feels natural in other languages, you'd reach for a shared visited set, a shared results array, pointers flying everywhere without much friction, and that's precisely the pattern Rust makes annoying on purpose. Fighting through that friction on a genuinely tree-shaped problem, rather than a toy example specifically designed to teach ownership in isolation, made the lesson land differently than it had in the book's more contrived examples so far. The book teaches ownership with small, clean examples, understandably, since it's introducing the concept from scratch, but small clean examples don't always transfer to messier real code the way this rustlings exercise's slightly awkward, more realistic shape actually did tonight. I'm going to flag this exercise specifically to the study group as a good "second pass" once everyone finishes the book chapter, since I suspect the same delayed click will happen for Lotte and Kofi too, just on whatever problem is messy enough to force the same reckoning with old habits.

## What's next

Kofi mentioned during the meetup that this exact realization, ownership stops being a fight and starts being a design tool once it clicks properly, is roughly when people start actually enjoying the language instead of just tolerating it to get through the tutorial. I want to test that theory on my next project, a small key-value store with more graph-like structure than the todo app had and should be a good stress test for whether tonight's shift in intuition actually holds up under slightly more real pressure than a rustlings exercise, or whether it quietly evaporates the moment the problem gets genuinely complicated instead of just moderately so. Either way, I'll write it down here regardless of which way it goes.
