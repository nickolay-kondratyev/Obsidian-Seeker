# Reading Notes: *The Color Line and the City*, Voss

Voss traces how 1930s redlining maps hardened into today's zoning boundaries. Dense, well-argued, occasionally repetitive.

## Core Claim

Racial zoning didn't vanish after being struck down; it re-encoded itself as lot-size rules (p. 41).

## Method

Voss pairs digitized HOLC redlining maps against modern zoning overlays across nine cities, tract by tract, from 1937 to 2020 (pp. 58-66).

## Key Quote

"The map outlived the law that drew it — the boundary was the point" (p. 73).

## Chapter 4 — The Single-Family Enclosure

Voss argues single-family-only zoning is the direct descendant of racial covenants, tracing how model ordinances spread city to city after 1917 (pp. 95-121). Strongest chapter in the book by far, and the one I keep coming back to and citing in my own drafts.

## Weaknesses

Case selection skews toward large Northern cities; Voss barely addresses Sunbelt annexation patterns, which [[157-literature-review-project]] will need to cover instead (p. 140).
