# Where Product Photography Is Actually Headed

Every few months someone forwards me an article predicting that product photography as a service is about to disappear, replaced by generative tools that can produce a photorealistic shot of a shoe or a bottle of shampoo from a text prompt in seconds. I've read enough of these now to have a real opinion, and it's more nuanced than either the doom articles or the "nothing will ever change" crowd on the photography forums want to admit. Some categories of this work genuinely are shrinking, and I'd be lying if I said I wasn't watching that shift closely from inside a small studio that depends on exactly the kind of bookings most at risk. But the shrinkage is concentrated in a specific, identifiable slice of the market, not spread evenly across everything I do, and understanding where that line actually falls matters more than having a strong opinion about AI in general. The clearest place I've seen the shift already happening is flat, simple, high-volume catalog work: a single product on a pure white background, consistent angle, minimal styling. That kind of image is exactly what current generative tools are good at, because there's very little for the model to get wrong when the reference is a clean product photo and the background is featureless. I've watched at least two smaller catalog-heavy clients quietly reduce their studio bookings over the past year in favor of AI-assisted backgrounds and even some fully synthetic product renders for early-stage listings, and I don't think that trend reverses. It's not really a photography problem specifically, it's the same pattern that's played out in a dozen other fields where a task is repetitive, well-specified, and produces a narrow, predictable range of acceptable outputs. When the target output is genuinely simple and consistent, automation eventually gets there, and pretending otherwise out of professional pride doesn't change the outcome, it just delays the moment a studio actually adapts to it.

## Where the Line Actually Falls

The work that's holding up, and in my own bookings actually growing, is anything where the input is physically unpredictable or where trust in accuracy genuinely matters to the buyer. Jewelry and reflective surfaces are a good example: the exact way light interacts with a specific stone's facets or a particular metal's brushed finish is close to impossible to fake convincingly yet, and buyers in that category are unusually sensitive to inaccuracy because the purchase decision is expensive and the product is small enough that tiny visual details carry real weight. Furniture and larger physical goods carry a similar dynamic for a different reason: buyers want to trust that the photograph shows the actual item, not a plausible approximation, especially for anything being bought sight-unseen for a home.

## Why Trust Is the Real Variable

The pattern isn't really about technical difficulty, even though that's part of it. It's about how much the buyer's confidence depends on the image being a faithful representation of a real object rather than a plausible rendering. A shopper buying a fifteen-dollar phone case doesn't need that guarantee and won't care if the photo is AI-assisted. A shopper choosing a hand-finished dining table or an engagement ring absolutely does need it, whether they'd articulate it that way or not, and that need doesn't seem to be going away just because the technology keeps improving at the easier end of the market. I noticed this clearly with a furniture client last year who asked me to keep a small visible scuff on a reclaimed-wood table in the final images, because removing it would have made the piece look mass-produced, and the imperfection was doing real persuasive work for exactly the buyer they wanted. No generative tool prompted with "reclaimed wood table" would have known to preserve that detail, since it isn't a stylistic choice, it's a fact about one physical object that only exists because someone photographed the real thing.

## What This Means for a Small Studio

Practically, this has already reshaped how I think about which bookings to prioritize, something I've written about more directly in [[183-year-two-reflections]]. I've been deliberately leaning into the jewelry and reflective-product side of the business rather than trying to compete on volume catalog work where the margin is thin and getting thinner as clients experiment with cheaper AI-assisted alternatives. That's not a purely defensive move either, the complex lighting work is also just more interesting to shoot and more differentiated as a skill, which matters for pricing power over the next several years regardless of how the automation story plays out further. I don't think this fully insulates a small studio from the broader shift. Prices for the simple end of the market will likely keep compressing, and the smart move is treating that as a signal to specialize rather than to fight it head-on. Studios that stay generalist, doing a bit of everything at a mid-tier price, are the ones I'd worry about most over the next five years, since they're competing against automation on the easy end and against better-differentiated specialists on the hard end simultaneously, with no clear advantage on either side of that squeeze. I've started saying this out loud to a couple of other local studio owners, partly to test the argument against pushback and partly because I think there's more value in a handful of small studios specializing into different, non-overlapping niches than there is in all of us quietly competing for the same shrinking pool of generalist catalog work as the easy end keeps getting automated out from under everyone at roughly the same pace.

## A Closing Thought

None of this is a prediction I'd bet the whole business on blindly, and I could be wrong about how fast the harder categories eventually fall too. But specializing toward trust-sensitive, physically unpredictable work seems like the more durable bet available right now.
