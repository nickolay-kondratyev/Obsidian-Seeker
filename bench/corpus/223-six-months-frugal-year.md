Six months into what Dana and I have been half-jokingly calling "the frugal year" and I wanted to actually sit down and take stock instead of just vaguely feeling like it's going fine, which is the trap I keep falling into with anything that doesn't have a hard weekly check-in attached to it. The frugal year wasn't a New Year's resolution exactly, more a response to looking at the [[212-debt-payoff-plan]] numbers back in December and realizing we could shave real months off both loans if we got serious about discretionary spending for a defined stretch rather than an open-ended forever commitment that would eventually collapse under its own weight the way every previous attempt at "just spending less" had within a few months of starting. Six months felt long enough to matter and short enough to actually sustain without burning out on it entirely, and so far that framing has held up better than I expected it to going in, though I'll admit there were a few weeks in March where the whole thing felt more like deprivation than deliberate choice, and I had to actively remind myself why we'd agreed to this in the first place, pulling up the original debt payoff numbers on my phone at the grocery store checkout like some kind of talisman against giving in to an impulse buy. What kept it from tipping into resentment, I think, was that we'd chosen the frugal year together rather than one of us imposing it on the other, so when the friction showed up it felt like a shared cost rather than something being done to me specifically.

## The numbers so far

The actual numbers: dining out spending down 40% from our 2025 average, subscription spending down to three services from the original seven, and the extra $400/month toward the car loan holding steady every single month without a single skip, even during a tighter week in February when it would have been easy to rationalize pulling back just this once. What surprised me most wasn't the discipline required, which honestly hasn't been that bad once the new habits set in around month two, but how little we actually miss most of what we cut. The streaming bundle we dropped, we haven't thought about once since March. The takeout habit, mostly replaced by meal prepping on Sundays, which took real effort to establish as a routine but now runs almost on autopilot most weeks. What we do still miss, genuinely, is the spontaneity of saying yes to a last-minute dinner invite without doing quick math first, and I don't think that tension fully resolves until the frugal year officially ends and we consciously decide what carries forward versus what reverts back to how things were before. There's also a version of this that worries me slightly: that we get so used to the tighter version of life that loosening back up feels wasteful even once the loans are gone and there's no longer a clear reason to keep cutting, which would be its own kind of problem, trading one imbalance for another instead of landing somewhere genuinely sustainable. Priyam warned me about exactly this when I mentioned the frugal year to her back in January, said she'd watched a few people in her FIRE circle get so identified with frugality as a virtue that they kept living well below their means for years after it stopped serving any actual purpose, treating restraint itself as the goal instead of a tool aimed at a specific, achievable target. I don't think we're there, but it's worth keeping an eye on, and probably worth naming explicitly at month twelve rather than assuming the transition back to normal spending will happen naturally on its own.

## What's actually different

Two loans are meaningfully smaller than they'd be without this, the car loan specifically about four months ahead of the original schedule from [[212-debt-payoff-plan]], which is the whole point and worth the friction on its own even before counting the softer changes. But the bigger shift might be discovering that our baseline spending had more slack in it than either of us assumed going in, meaning some fraction of these cuts probably becomes permanent even after the defined frugal-year window closes at the end of the year, not because we're forcing it to stay that way but because the old habits genuinely don't feel worth going back to now that we've lived without them for a while. The emergency fund from [[217-emergency-fund-rebuild-plan]] has also benefited quietly, since the same discretionary discipline that's accelerating the car loan naturally makes the fund's monthly transfer feel smaller and less noticeable than it would have back in January. Even our grocery spending, which we didn't explicitly target, has drifted down slightly just from cooking more often at home instead of ordering out on tired weeknights.

## What happens at month twelve

We haven't decided yet whether to extend, taper gradually back to normal spending, or declare it done and consciously choose which cuts stick. Revisiting that question properly at the [[216-annual-financial-planning-meeting]] equivalent for this specific commitment, sometime in December, once we have the full year of data in front of us instead of guessing from six months of a trend that could still shift in either direction before then. Whatever we decide, I want to write it down deliberately rather than let it happen by default the way the travel budget quietly rolled into savings last year without either of us choosing that outcome on purpose. Setting a reminder now, months ahead, feels like the only reliable way to make sure December-us actually revisits this instead of letting the frugal year quietly become the permanent year without a real conversation.
