# Fixing a Flat by the Roadside

Every member should be able to do this without waiting for a marshal, because sooner or later you'll flat far from the clubhouse with nobody around. This is the method taught at the spring maintenance clinic, using a spare tube rather than patching on the roadside, which is faster and less fiddly with cold hands.

## Step by Step

First, get off the road onto grass or a verge before you do anything else; changing a tube on the white line of a country road is how people get hit, and it happens more often than the statistics suggest anyone should be comfortable with. Flip the bike upside down or lean it against something stable, then shift into the smallest cog if you're dealing with a rear wheel, which makes the wheel easier to seat back into the dropouts later. Open the brake if it's a rim brake, release the quick-release or loosen the thru-axle, and lift the wheel free. Don't rush this part; a wheel dropped on gravel can bend a rotor or scuff a rim, and you don't want a second problem stacked on the first.

Deflate the tyre fully if there's any air left, then work one bead off the rim using your thumbs first, starting opposite the valve. If the tyre is stubborn, this is where tyre levers earn their keep: hook one under the bead, pop it over the rim, then slide a second lever around the circumference rather than trying to lever the whole thing off in one spot. Pull the old tube out and set it aside; don't throw it away yet, because a small hole can often be patched at home later even if you don't want to bother with it on the roadside. Run two fingers carefully around the inside of the tyre, checking for the thorn, wire, or shard of glass that caused the flat. Skipping this step is the single most common reason for a second flat ten minutes after the first; whatever caused the puncture is usually still there unless it fell out on its own; check the outside of the tyre too, since a visible cut or embedded flint is easy to miss if you only feel from the inside.

Once you're confident the tyre is clear, partially inflate the new tube with a few strokes of a hand pump or a couple of seconds of CO2, just enough that it holds a rough shape without ballooning. This makes it far less likely to twist or pinch as you install it. Tuck the valve through the rim hole first, then work the tube into the tyre by hand, going around evenly rather than stuffing one section in fully before moving on. With the tube seated, push one bead of the tyre back onto the rim, going around both directions from the valve, and finish with the second bead, using your thumbs and saving the levers as a last resort since they're the most common way to pinch a fresh tube and flat it a second time before you've even finished the job.

Before fully inflating, check that the tyre is seated evenly by spinning the wheel and watching the gap between the rim and the tyre wall; an uneven wobble usually means the tube is pinched somewhere underneath. Inflate slowly at first, stopping around ten psi to double-check the seating, then finish to your usual pressure. Reinstall the wheel, making sure it's centred and the quick-release or thru-axle is properly tight, reconnect the brake if you opened it, and give the wheel a spin to confirm nothing is rubbing. Pack the old tube, tyre levers, and pump away, and you're back on the road, ideally in well under ten minutes once you've practiced this a handful of times at home rather than learning it cold on the roadside for the first time.

## Kit To Carry

At minimum: one spare tube matching your tyre size and valve type, two tyre levers, and either a compact hand pump or two CO2 cartridges with an inflator head. A small multi-tool covers most other roadside problems. Store it all in a saddle bag you never have to think about, so it's simply there when needed, rather than something to remember before every ride. Keep a spare tube even if you run tubeless, since sealant handles most punctures but not sidewall cuts.
