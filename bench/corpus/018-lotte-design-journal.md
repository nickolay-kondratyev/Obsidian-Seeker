I keep coming back to the invoice detail screen. Priyam shipped the revision-history redesign last week and it's *technically* correct — every draft revision is listed, timestamps are right, the diff between revisions renders cleanly — but when I watch a new user look at it for the first time, there's a beat of confusion before they understand why there are three "invoice #1042" rows stacked on top of each other. The immutability model makes total sense to us because we've lived inside it for months, but a first-time user has no mental model for "sent invoices can't be edited, so this is a new draft" until someone explains it. I don't think the fix is more UI chrome. I think it's a single well-placed sentence the first time someone hits this screen, and then getting out of the way. It's the kind of problem that's easy to miss internally precisely because we're the worst possible judges of it — none of us can un-know how the system works long enough to see the screen the way a first-time user does.

## Watching the onboarding session

Sat in on a session with a new customer today, a small landscaping business owner setting up her first few invoices. She got through customer creation and the first invoice draft without hesitation — the flow there is genuinely smooth, no notes. Where she stalled was exactly where I expected: she edited a sent invoice, expecting the change to apply in place, and instead got a new draft revision with no explanation of what had just happened or why. She asked out loud, "did that not save?" which is the worst possible read of correct behavior — the system did exactly the right thing and she experienced it as a bug. I watched her re-check the customer's email to see if a duplicate invoice had gone out, which it hadn't, but the fact that she felt the need to check is the whole problem in one gesture. Kofi was in the room too and pointed out this is the second time this exact confusion has come up in a session; I want to pull the notes from the first one before I propose anything. What struck me most was how quickly her confidence dropped after that moment — she'd been moving fast and comfortably right up until then, and afterward she double-checked every subsequent action, hovering over buttons longer, visibly less willing to trust that things were working the way she expected. One confusing moment early in onboarding seems to cost more than its own weight; it seeds doubt that colors everything after it, and that's the part I keep thinking about days later.

## Options I'm weighing

A one-time inline explainer the first time a user edits a sent invoice, versus a persistent small label on revision rows, versus renaming "draft revision" to something less jargony like "updated copy." None of these are mutually exclusive, but I want to test the cheapest one first. There's also a fourth option I keep talking myself out of: doing nothing and trusting repeated exposure to teach the model over time. I don't trust that option — the cost of one bad first impression outweighs whatever time it saves.

## Where I've landed for now

The rename is tempting but risky — "draft revision" is precise and matches language we use internally in support docs and in [[015-reading-notes-invoicing-paper]], and if I rename it in the UI without renaming it everywhere, we get a worse problem: two names for the same concept depending on where you're looking, which is arguably more confusing than the current single-name confusion. So that's out for now, or at least out until I'm willing to touch the support docs too, and right now I'm not, given everything else on the roadmap this quarter.

The inline explainer feels right as a first experiment. Small, temporary, dismissible, shown exactly once per org the first time someone edits a sent invoice, gone forever after that — something like "This invoice was already sent, so your changes are saved as a new draft. The original stays unchanged." One sentence, plain language, no jargon about immutability or revisions as a concept. I sketched three variants this afternoon, one more clinical and two more conversational, and I want Priyam's read on implementation cost before I commit to testing all three against real users versus just picking the strongest one and shipping it. My instinct says ship the conversational one and watch the next few onboarding sessions rather than running a formal test — we don't have the user volume yet for a real A/B split to tell us much, and waiting for statistical confidence here would just mean shipping nothing for a month while the same confusion keeps happening in every session, over and over, with no one actually doing anything about it in the meantime. I'd rather ship a reasonable guess this week and revise it based on what I actually observe than wait for data we can't realistically get yet given our current volume. Going to write this up properly and bring it to Priyam tomorrow instead of just dropping a Slack message about it — this deserves an actual conversation, not a drive-by request buried in a thread he'll see three days late.
