New playtesters join the rotation every few months, and a bad first session tends to produce vague or overly polite feedback instead of useful signal. This guide walks through getting a first-timer from "never seen the box" to "giving notes we can actually act on" in one sitting, without overwhelming them with rules text before they've touched a single component.

## Running a First Session

Start before the table is even set up. Send the new playtester a two-line summary a day ahead of time: what Tidewatch is (a cooperative game where lighthouse keepers race rising water and storms), how long a session runs, and that they don't need to read anything beforehand. Reading the rulebook cold before ever seeing the components tends to confuse people more than it helps, since Tidewatch has several interlocking systems, the tide track, the storm deck, and keeper actions, that make far more sense once you can point at the physical board while explaining them. Kofi has run this onboarding dozens of times and insists that the single biggest mistake is over-preparing a new player with theory before they've touched a single tide token.

When they arrive, don't hand them the rulebook. Set up the board yourself while narrating what each piece does, in plain language rather than rules-speak: "this track shows how high the water is, and every turn it usually goes up by one unless we manage to hold it back." Keep the first setup narration under five minutes; longer than that and people start nodding along without actually absorbing anything, which shows up later as confused decisions rather than honest confusion you can address in the moment.

Play the first storm phase slowly and out loud. Narrate your own reasoning as the facilitator on your first turn: "I'm moving my keeper to the seawall because the storm card showed a surge coming from the north, and if I don't reinforce it now, next turn's flood will hit two spaces instead of one." New players learn the game's logic far faster from watching one demonstrated turn than from any amount of rules text, because Tidewatch's core tension, spend actions now to prevent a worse problem later, is intuitive once modeled but genuinely hard to explain in the abstract.

Let mistakes happen in the first two turns without correcting them immediately, unless a mistake would make the rest of the session unplayable, like miscounting the storm track. A misplayed action that just wastes a turn is a fine, low-cost way for someone to learn where their options actually are, and stopping every small error breaks immersion and makes new players self-conscious about asking questions later, right when curiosity matters most for a useful first session.

Around turn four or five, once the group has weathered one storm surge together, pause and ask a targeted question rather than an open one: "was there a moment just now where you weren't sure what your options were?" Open questions like "any thoughts?" tend to produce polite nothing-answers from people who don't yet have the vocabulary to describe what confused them, while specific, recent, moment-anchored questions produce useful, actionable notes almost every time, since the confusion is still fresh rather than already forgotten or rationalized away by the time the session ends.

Finish the session even if the group loses, and especially if they lose in an interesting way, since near-losses tend to generate the richest feedback of any outcome. A crushing early loss from an unlucky storm draw is worth noting as a possible balance issue, but don't over-index on one unlucky session, since Tidewatch's storm deck has enough variance that any single early game can run hot or cold. After the game ends, ask the new playtester to describe, in their own words and without prompting toward any particular rule, the moment during the session where the tension felt highest, and separately, the moment where they felt least sure what they were supposed to be doing. Those two answers together tend to be worth more than a full page of general impressions, because they point at specific turns you can go back and examine rather than vague overall sentiment about whether the session felt fun, which is real feedback but much harder to act on directly.

Write their answers down immediately, before the next session or the next conversation replaces the details in your memory, and log the session using the same format described in [[274-how-to-run-tidewatch-playtest]] so the note is comparable against other sessions later.

## After the Session

Send a short thank-you note within a day, along with a one-line summary of any change their feedback is already influencing, even a small one; new playtesters who see their notes actually matter tend to come back and give sharper feedback the second time. Add them to the group chat only if they want a longer-term role, since some people are happy to help once and would rather not be looped into ongoing planning discussions about [[292-tidewatch-kickstarter-prep]] or manufacturing timelines they have no stake in.
