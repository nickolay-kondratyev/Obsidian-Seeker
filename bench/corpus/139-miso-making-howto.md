Homemade miso takes months to mature but only about an hour of hands-on work, mostly upfront. This is the basic soybean-koji method I've used twice with reliable results.

## Ingredients

Dried soybeans, rice koji, and non-iodized salt, roughly in a 1:1:0.5 ratio by weight once the beans are cooked and drained. Iodized salt can inhibit fermentation and slow the whole batch down, so check the box first before starting anything else.

## Soak and cook

Soak beans overnight, then simmer until a bean mashes easily between two fingers.

## Mash

Mash the cooked beans while still warm, either by hand with a potato masher or briefly in a food processor, aiming for a mostly smooth paste with a few small lumps left rather than a completely uniform puree, which apparently helps texture develop properly during the long fermentation ahead. Reserve some cooking liquid in case the mash needs loosening later.

## Mix in koji and salt

Once the mash cools to lukewarm, mix in the koji and most of the salt by hand, reserving a small handful for later.

## Pack and seal

Form the mash into fist-sized balls and pack firmly into a clean crock, pressing out air pockets between additions. Smooth the top, sprinkle the reserved salt as a mold barrier, press plastic wrap against it, then weight with a stone.

## Wait

Store cool and dark for six to twelve months, checking for surface mold now and then.

## Troubleshooting

A thin layer of dark liquid pooling on top, called tamari, is a good sign rather than a problem — some batches produce more than others, and it can be spooned off and used as a seasoning liquid or stirred back in once fermentation is done. White or grey fuzzy mold on the surface is normal and gets scraped away; anything green, black, or pink means the batch should be discarded. If the paste smells sharply of alcohol rather than savory, it likely needs longer, not less, before it's ready to taste.
