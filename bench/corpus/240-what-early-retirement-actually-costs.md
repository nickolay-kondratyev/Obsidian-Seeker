I keep coming back to this because every time I run our numbers I discover I was quietly underestimating something. The FIRE calculators online make it look clean: multiply annual spending by twenty-five, hit that number, you're done. But actual early retirement, the kind that starts in your forties instead of your sixties, has a handful of cost categories that behave very differently from a normal retirement, and most of the popular shortcuts either ignore them or wave them away with an assumption that doesn't hold up under scrutiny. This is my attempt to lay out, category by category, what I think the honest cost of an early retirement actually looks like for a couple like us, based on real numbers we've gathered over the last year rather than a rule of thumb borrowed from a blog post. Some of this is uncomfortable. The healthcare number in particular came in higher than the estimate I'd been carrying around for two years, which is part of what prompted me to sit down and actually price it out properly instead of continuing to guess, a discomfort I wrote about more personally in [[213-early-retirement-doubts]] after the number first landed. The core problem with most retirement math is that it's built for people retiring at sixty-five into Medicare and a shrinking time horizon, and early retirement breaks both of those assumptions at once — you need to bridge a much longer gap before any public healthcare system kicks in, and your money needs to survive decades longer than the standard thirty-year retirement models were built to handle, which changes the shape of the risk considerably. None of that means early retirement is a bad idea. It means the honest cost is higher and lumpier than the clean multiply-by-twenty-five number suggests, and pretending otherwise just moves the reckoning to a worse moment, later, when there's less room to adjust, less time left to earn more, and fewer good options for closing a gap that could have been sized correctly years earlier.

## Healthcare: the number that surprised us

This is the category where our early estimate was most wrong. We'd been budgeting roughly $600 a month for health insurance for the two of us, a rough plan glanced at years ago, and when I priced out a comparable marketplace plan at our projected post-retirement income level, the real number came in closer to $1,100 a month before any actual healthcare usage. Premiums are only part of it — deductibles, out-of-pocket maximums, and no employer coverage to fall back on all push the realistic number higher than the premium sticker price. We're now budgeting closer to $1,400 a month once you include a reasonable usage allowance, nearly triple what I'd carried around for years, and it alone moves our target date by roughly a year and a half.

## Housing and the downsizing question

Housing costs shift meaningfully depending on whether we stay in our current place or downsize, a decision we're actively working through in [[232-downsizing-house-project]]. Property tax, maintenance, and insurance don't stop just because you've stopped earning a paycheck, and a paid-off house still costs real money every year to keep running — roof, furnace, appliances, the slow accumulation of things that eventually need replacing whether or not there's a mortgage attached to them anymore.

If we downsize, the math changes twice over: lower ongoing carrying costs, and a chunk of equity released to add directly to the portfolio, which is the scenario Dana has been modeling in detail lately. If we stay, we need to budget more conservatively for the deferred maintenance list we've been putting off precisely because we're not sure yet which way this decision goes, and every year of deferral makes the eventual bill larger rather than smaller. Either path is workable financially. What isn't workable is leaving the decision unmade indefinitely while budgeting as though we've already chosen the cheaper of the two outcomes.

## Lifestyle inflation and sequence-of-returns risk

The quieter risk is that spending creeps upward once there's more free time to fill and no commute eating into the day. A retired schedule opens space for hobbies, travel, and social spending that didn't show up in the working-years budget, and it's easy to justify each new expense individually while missing the cumulative drift across a year. We're addressing this partly through the zero-based approach in [[214-build-a-zero-based-budget]], rebuilt annually rather than assumed constant, specifically so lifestyle creep gets caught early instead of discovered five years in when the math has already quietly shifted underneath us without anyone noticing in the moment.

The other risk isn't a monthly line item at all — it's the danger of a market downturn in the first few years after quitting, when withdrawals from a smaller portfolio do outsized permanent damage compared to the identical downturn hitting later in retirement, after compounding has had more time to build a larger cushion. Two portfolios with identical average returns over thirty years can produce wildly different outcomes depending purely on the order those returns arrive in, which is genuinely counterintuitive the first time you see it modeled out. This is why we're building a cash and short-term bond buffer specifically sized to cover several years of spending without touching equities during a downturn, an idea we're still evaluating seriously via [[220-barbell-investing-strategy]] rather than something we've fully committed to yet, but that's looking increasingly necessary the more we read about how badly early sequence risk can compound.

## Where this leaves our number

Running healthcare, housing, a lifestyle-creep buffer, and a sequence-risk cash cushion through the full projection pushes our real target meaningfully above the naive twenty-five-times number, and honestly further out than either of us wants it to be.
