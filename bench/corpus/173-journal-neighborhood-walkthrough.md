# 2026-08-29, Saturday

Spent four hours this morning walking the Elm Corridor from the transit terminal down to the old rail yard, doing the observational pass I'd been putting off for two weeks. Weather was good for it — overcast, low seventies, nobody rushing anywhere because of heat. I brought a notebook instead of typing into my phone, partly because people react differently to a phone versus a pad of paper, and partly because I wanted to slow myself down. It worked, mostly. I ended up sitting on a bus bench near the old laundromat for almost twenty minutes just watching who came and went, longer than I usually let myself linger anywhere.

The corridor has changed more than I expected since I last did a systematic walk here in the spring. Two storefronts that were vacant in April now have permit notices taped up, which tracks with what Priyam mentioned at the [[166-meeting-housing-coalition]] meeting about a small wave of new leases. I counted roughly a dozen buildings under visible renovation between the terminal and 9th, more than I remember from any previous walk. Scaffolding on the old furniture warehouse looks like it's been up for months based on the rust on the joints, its own kind of data point about how slow some of this actually moves despite how fast it can feel from the outside.

I kept coming back to something from [[175-reading-notes-color-line-city]] — the argument that the physical grain of a block can outlive the policy that produced it. You can see the old lot lines here even where the buildings on top of them have been replaced three or four times.

## Observations Log

I kept a rough table this time instead of just narrative notes, mostly to force myself to be more consistent about noting the time and not just vibes.

| Site | Time | Note |
|------|------|------|
| Transit terminal plaza | 9:05am | Sparse foot traffic, one vendor cart set up, no line |
| Corner of Elm & 9th | 9:40am | Three storefronts with new permit notices in windows |
| Old laundromat bench | 10:05am | Sat and observed; mostly older residents, dog walkers |
| Rail yard fence line | 10:50am | New graffiti mural, unrelated to fieldwork but notable |
| Community garden plots | 11:20am | Two plots visibly untended, one being actively watered |
| Bus stop near clinic | 11:45am | Long wait observed for the 14 line, nine people queued |

The bus stop entry is the one I want to follow up on. Nine people waiting at 11:45 on a Saturday for a route that's supposed to run every fifteen minutes felt like more than routine variance, and it lines up with a complaint pattern I've seen mentioned in passing at the transit agency interview notes. I don't want to overread one observation, but it's worth flagging for the survey design work, especially since ridership complaints tend to get dismissed as anecdotal unless someone actually times the gaps and writes them down in a way that can be compared across weeks.

I also noticed how much quieter the community garden was compared to my spring visit — two of the plots had gone visibly untended, which could mean anything from a family moving away to just a busy summer, but it's the kind of small signal that's easy to miss if you're only looking at listing data or permit filings and never actually walking the block. The vendor cart at the terminal plaza was new too; I don't remember seeing any street vending there in April, which might just be seasonal but is worth a note regardless, since informal commerce is one of the things the coalition keeps saying gets squeezed out first when rents start climbing along a corridor like this one.

## Reflections

Doing this on foot instead of from a spreadsheet keeps reminding me why I chose this method in the first place, even when it eats an entire Saturday morning. The permit notices tell me something is changing, but only the bench sitting told me who's still there right now, today, before whatever comes next actually arrives. I don't think I can fully operationalize that distinction for the dissertation, but I want to keep trying, because it feels like the whole argument, even if my committee keeps pushing me toward something more countable.

I need to do at least two more of these walks before the weather turns, one on a weekday morning to compare foot traffic patterns, and one more on transit specifically, timing headways at three stops instead of just noting one long wait anecdotally. Part of me also wants to walk the corridor at dusk once, just to see whether the same storefronts and benches read differently once the light changes and the people using the street change with it.
