# Why we chose a barbell investing strategy

A barbell strategy puts money at the two ends of the risk spectrum and mostly skips the middle: a large chunk in very safe, liquid assets, and a smaller chunk in higher-risk, higher-return assets, with little to nothing in the moderate-risk middle ground that most target-date funds default to. We landed on a version of this a year and a half ago after getting uncomfortable with how much of our portfolio sat in funds that were neither safe enough to sleep on nor aggressive enough to justify the volatility, a mushy middle that captured downside risk without capturing much of the upside that was supposedly the point of taking risk in the first place. The idea isn't original to us; it's borrowed loosely from writing on antifragility, and we adapted it specifically for a long early-retirement runway rather than short-term trading, which changes the math considerably. Our version is roughly 70% broad low-cost index funds and cash-equivalent safety, and 30% in higher-conviction individual positions and a small allocation to more speculative assets we're comfortable losing entirely. The safe end includes short-term treasuries and the emergency fund itself, both explicitly zero-drama by design. The risk end is capped hard: if it grows to more than 35% of the portfolio through gains rather than new contributions, we trim back to target rather than letting winners run indefinitely and quietly reshape our actual risk exposure. We sketched this on a napkin during a slow Sunday, moved it into a spreadsheet the following week, and have kept it essentially unchanged since. Dana was skeptical at first, worried the risk bucket was just a fancier name for speculation dressed up in a framework that made it sound more disciplined than it actually was, and that worry wasn't unreasonable at the time. It took a few months of watching the safe bucket behave exactly as boring as promised before she came around to the idea that the discipline lived in the structure itself. What won her over wasn't a return number, it was the written rules: the trim threshold, the review cadence, and the explicit list of things we'd deliberately excluded.

## The math behind it

The core claim behind a barbell isn't that it beats a diversified middle-risk portfolio on average returns, it's that it changes the shape of the outcome distribution in a way that matters more to us than the average. A pure 100% safe portfolio guarantees mediocrity; a pure 100% aggressive portfolio has real ruin risk if the timing goes badly, particularly the kind of sequence-of-returns risk that matters most in the years right around an early retirement date. The barbell caps the downside hard through the safe half while still leaving room for real upside through the risk half, without ever exposing the whole portfolio to a single bad regime in the same way a uniformly moderate-risk portfolio can when the entire market has a mediocre decade.

## What's in each bucket, and what we left out

Safe bucket: emergency fund, a short-term treasury ladder, and a broad low-cost index fund held specifically for its liquidity. Risk bucket: a handful of individual positions we've researched carefully and are willing to hold through a 50%+ drawdown without flinching, plus a small allocation, capped at 5% of total portfolio, to more speculative assets purely for asymmetric upside. Deliberately left out: no actively managed mutual funds, no individual bonds beyond the treasury ladder, no leveraged products, and nothing that requires more than a quarterly check-in to maintain, since the point is reducing ongoing decision fatigue, not adding a new hobby of tinkering. We also skip sector-specific funds entirely, since they blur the clean line between the two buckets. Real estate investment trusts didn't make the cut either: too correlated with equities to feel truly safe, too diluted to deliver the asymmetric upside we want from the risk side. Cryptocurrency lives inside the speculative sub-allocation, capped tightly, treated the same as any other position we've mentally already written off.

## Where this could go wrong

The most obvious failure mode is behavioral rather than mathematical: watching the risk bucket occasionally underperform will tempt us to abandon it right when patience matters most, usually near a bottom rather than a top, which is exactly when the emotional pull to "just get safe" is strongest and exactly when acting on that pull tends to lock in the worst possible outcome. We've written the 35%-trim rule down explicitly, here and in the shared spreadsheet, specifically so a future version of us with a shorter memory and more fear in the moment can't quietly rationalize deviating from it during a genuinely scary month. There's also a structural risk that the "safe" bucket isn't as safe as it feels: treasuries carry interest-rate risk even if they're default-risk-free, and a rate spike could produce a real, if temporary, mark-to-market loss on the ladder that feels uncomfortably close to the volatility we built the safe side specifically to avoid. And the risk bucket's individual positions carry concentration risk that a broad index fund simply doesn't, which is the tradeoff we've accepted deliberately but need to keep re-examining rather than treating as settled once and for all, since conviction that felt well-reasoned eighteen months ago can quietly calcify into stubbornness if we're not honest with ourselves about it. We've agreed to treat any position we can't articulate a fresh reason for holding as a candidate for trimming, regardless of how it's performed. Finally, there's the risk that the whole framework is just an elaborate story we're telling ourselves to feel better about volatility we'd tolerate anyway, and that possibility is worth sitting with honestly rather than dismissing simply because the spreadsheet looks tidy.
