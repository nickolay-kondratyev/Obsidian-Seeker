Three hours at the desk today and I have maybe four usable sentences to show for it. The methods chapter is supposed to be the easy one — I know what I did, I have the code, I have the panel, I just cannot find the shape of the argument. Every paragraph I start feels like it's explaining the wrong thing first. I keep opening the outline doc, staring at "3.2 Sampling Strategy," and closing it again to check email instead. Dana texted asking how the writing was going and I said "fine," which is a lie I am now also telling this journal, apparently, out of habit. I know the fix isn't more coffee or a new outline template, but at ten in the morning those still feel like more productive activities than actually opening the document. By eleven I had refilled the kettle twice, rewritten the outline heading three different ways, and not typed a single sentence of actual prose into the chapter file itself.

## Who am I even writing this for

I think part of the problem is that I still don't know who I'm writing this chapter for. My committee already knows the survey design cold, they sat through two presentations on it back in the fall, including the messy version where the pilot response rate came in low and we argued for forty minutes about incentive amounts. So am I writing for them, or for some hypothetical future reader who's never heard of the project and needs every term defined from scratch? Those are genuinely different chapters, with different pacing and different things left implicit. I've been trying to write both at once for the past two weeks and predictably getting neither one done, just a document that's too slow for the committee and too assumption-heavy for anyone else, a chapter that satisfies nobody in particular while trying politely to satisfy everybody. Naming this out loud, even just to myself in this entry, already makes tomorrow's draft feel more possible than it did this morning, which is annoying because it took two weeks to notice, and because I'm fairly sure I made the exact same observation about the intro chapter back in October and then promptly forgot it. Priyam says every chapter teaches you this lesson fresh, as if the previous chapter's version of you left no notes for the next one, which tracks, since I genuinely cannot find where past-me wrote any of this down, if she ever did.

## Today's procrastination, catalogued

The procrastination today took its usual shape: reorganizing the Zotero library instead of citing anything in it, then reorganizing the folder structure for the cleaning scripts even though nothing about them actually changed. None of it was wasted exactly, the Zotero tags are cleaner now, but none of it was the chapter either, and by four o'clock I had opened the document eleven separate times and written, net, about sixty words, most of which I deleted again by five.

## What actually worked

What actually helped, eventually, was giving up on writing the chapter directly and just talking to myself in a scratch file about why the sampling strategy mattered in the first place. Not "here is the sampling strategy" but "here is why I didn't just survey everyone at every stop, here is the specific tradeoff I was making between coverage and respondent fatigue, here is what Priyam and I argued about for a week in October." Once I wrote that down as an argument instead of a procedure, the actual methods section started following behind it almost mechanically — the constraints justify the choices, the choices justify the procedure, and the procedure is what actually goes in the chapter, dressed up and de-personalized. I got about four hundred words out of that scratch file that I would actually keep, which is more than the previous four days combined, and for the first time in a while I closed the laptop without that low grinding feeling that I'd lost another day to the chapter, instead of the usual dread about tomorrow's blank page staring back at me the same way.

I think the lesson, which I have apparently had to relearn three separate times this year already, is that I cannot draft a methods section directly, cold, from an outline. I have to draft the reasoning first, in a document nobody will ever see, full of asides and half-formed complaints about the pilot response rate, and only then is the methods section just the tidy version of that reasoning with the doubts and the swearing removed, the arguing-with-Priyam parts trimmed down to a single reasonable-sounding sentence about tradeoffs. Kofi mentioned at the conference in November that his advisor calls this the "ugly draft," and I remember dismissing it at the time as an obvious thing everyone already knows, and here I am rediscovering it myself in February at nine at night, slightly embarrassed about how long it took.

## Small win

Small win, but I'll take it. Tomorrow: turn the scratch file into actual section 3.2 prose, then see if 3.3 opens up the same way once there's some momentum behind it. Also, stop checking whether Dana replied about dinner every twenty minutes — that isn't helping the word count either, and I already know the answer is probably just "sure, seven?".
