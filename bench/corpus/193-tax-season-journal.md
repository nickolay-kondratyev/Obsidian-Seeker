Sat down this weekend to actually finish pulling everything together for the accountant, and for the first time in three years of doing this I didn't dread the process, which feels worth writing down before the feeling fades and next year I forget it was ever different. Part of it is just that the weekly bookkeeping habit I started last spring actually held for the whole year this time, categorizing expenses and matching receipts every Sunday evening instead of letting three months pile up and then losing an entire weekend to a frantic scramble every April. Part of it is also that the business itself is simpler to explain on paper than it used to be, one main studio location for most of the year, a predictable set of recurring clients, and gear purchases that are mostly maintenance rather than the constant flurry of experimental buys from year one that made last year's categorizing an absolute nightmare of trying to remember what half of them were even for eighteen months later. I still found a few things that needed cleaning up, an equipment purchase from October that got miscategorized as a studio supply instead of a depreciable asset, and one client payment that showed up twice in my tracking because I'd logged it manually before the invoicing tool's automatic sync caught it too, but nothing that took more than twenty minutes to sort out once I actually sat down and looked closely instead of putting it off yet again the way I would have in previous years.

## The Actual Numbers

Total revenue for the year came in at just over $61,000, up from about $43,000 the year before, which is a real jump but not quite as dramatic as it feels emotionally, since a decent chunk of that growth is really just the retainer-style bookings, see [[186-client-retainer-negotiation]], smoothing out what used to be much lumpier months rather than pure new growth on top of the old pattern. Studio rent and utilities came to just under $14,000 for the year, which will jump meaningfully once the relocation goes through this spring, something I need to budget for carefully rather than being surprised by it in next year's numbers the way I definitely would be if I didn't flag it now while I'm already looking at the full picture. Equipment and maintenance totaled around $3,800, lower than I expected going in, mostly because I made a real effort this year to stop impulse-buying gear and started asking whether a purchase would actually change what I could deliver before clicking buy, a habit that's paid off in ways beyond just the dollar figure. Software and subscriptions, editing tools, the invoicing platform, cloud backup, came to about $2,200, a line item I hadn't really tracked carefully before this year and was mildly surprised by once I added it all up in one place instead of letting it stay scattered across a dozen small recurring charges I barely noticed month to month. After deductions, the estimated tax owed looks manageable given what I'd already set aside in the quarterly savings account, which is itself a genuinely new habit for this year, previous years I paid quarterly estimates more or less on faith without much of a real cushion behind the number, and it's a relief to not be doing that kind of math under real pressure this time around. Travel costs for the two out-of-town furniture shoots added another $900, mostly flights and the rental car, which I'd nearly forgotten to include until I cross-checked the calendar against the receipts folder and caught the gap before it became a missing deduction I'd only notice after filing.

## What I'll Change Next Year

A few adjustments for next year based on what I ran into this weekend. First, I want a clearer system for flagging equipment purchases as depreciable assets right at the moment of purchase rather than during the year-end review, since that's where the miscategorization happened and it would take about thirty extra seconds at the point of sale to get it right the first time instead of hunting it down months later. Second, I want to separate the studio relocation costs into their own tracking category starting now, before the move actually happens, rather than lumping them in with regular studio expenses and having to untangle the two later when the accountant inevitably asks which costs were one-time versus ongoing. Third, I think it's worth scheduling an actual mid-year check-in with the accountant instead of only talking to them once a year at tax time, purely reactively, since a fifteen-minute call in July could probably catch small issues like the double-logged payment months before they'd otherwise surface.

## One Honest Note

I used to treat bookkeeping as a chore to survive rather than a real part of running the business, and this year is the first time it's actually felt like a tool that tells me something true and useful about the year rather than just a box to check for the accountant's sake. Looking at the revenue-by-client breakdown side by side with how much time each client actually took taught me more about where my energy is well spent than any single reflective journal entry has managed to on its own, numbers made a pattern obvious that feeling my way through the year never quite surfaced. I'm going to try pulling this same breakdown quarterly instead of once a year, so the lesson shapes decisions in real time rather than arriving as a wistful realization every April.
