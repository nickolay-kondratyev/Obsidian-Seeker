Launch day. Ledgerline went live for our first three paying customers this morning, and I've been staring at the dashboard metrics for so long my eyes hurt, in a good way. We soft-launched at 8am with Kofi watching error rates and me watching the job queue, since recurring invoice generation was the piece I trusted least under real load rather than the seeded data we'd been testing against for months. By 9am all three organizations had logged in, created at least one invoice, and nothing had caught fire, which after a year of building this in evenings and weekends before finally going full-time three months ago feels almost anticlimactic in a way I wasn't prepared for. I kept expecting the moment to feel bigger. Instead it felt like every other Tuesday, just with higher stakes and Kofi refreshing a dashboard every ninety seconds instead of every five minutes like usual when he's only mildly anxious. Lotte spent the morning on a call with our first customer, a small landscaping business run by a woman who found us through a mutual connection, walking her through creating a real invoice for an actual client rather than test data. It went smoothly enough that the customer sent a thumbs-up emoji mid-call, which Lotte screenshotted into our team channel with more excitement than the moment probably warranted, but none of us cared because it was the first real, non-simulated proof that a stranger could get value from something we built without anyone explaining every button.

## The one scare

Around 11am we had our one real scare of the day. A recurring invoice job failed silently for about four minutes before Kofi's alert fired, and for those four minutes I genuinely thought we might have a customer-facing incident on launch day of all days, which felt like exactly the kind of story people tell years later about how badly something almost went, half proud and half mortified in retrospect. The root cause turned out to be a null timezone on one test organization we'd forgotten to clean up from staging data that had somehow leaked into what we thought was a fresh production database, and the job crashed trying to format a date against `undefined` rather than a real value. Kofi killed the stray organization row within minutes once we found it, redeployed a defensive null check as a hotfix, and the whole thing resolved before any real customer noticed anything, since none of our three actual launch customers had triggered a recurring invoice yet at that point in the day, which was pure luck rather than anything we'd planned for. Still, my heart rate did not appreciate those four minutes, and I've already opened a ticket to add proper environment isolation checks to the deploy pipeline so a staging artifact can never end up in production data again, no matter how it happened this time or whose fault it technically was. Kofi pointed out, correctly and a little smugly, that this is exactly the class of bug our test suite from [[022-testing-strategy]] doesn't cover yet, since it wasn't really a code bug so much as a data hygiene failure that no unit or integration test would have caught on its own, and we spent fifteen minutes after things calmed down actually agreeing on what a build-time or deploy-time check for this specific failure mode would look like, sketched roughly on a whiteboard photo Kofi took before either of us went back to watching dashboards nervously for the rest of the afternoon, trading updates every twenty minutes whether or not there was anything new to report.

## What I'm feeling tonight

Tired, mostly, and quietly proud in a way that hasn't fully landed yet and probably won't until I've slept properly for the first time in what feels like weeks. There's a version of this year I could tell where nothing about today was inevitable — where we picked the wrong stack, or Kofi burned out from being the only ops-minded person on a four-person team, or Lotte's read on what small businesses actually need turned out to be wrong, or Priyam and I clashed hard enough over frontend architecture early on that one of us walked away. None of that happened, or at least not badly enough to stop us, and today three real people used something we built to do a genuinely important part of running their business. That's not nothing, even if the metrics dashboard makes it look like a small, unremarkable Tuesday to anyone glancing at it from outside without knowing what it took to get here, or how many smaller decisions along the way could have quietly derailed it.
