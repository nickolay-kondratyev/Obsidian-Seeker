## Attendees

- Facilitator: Dana, coalition organizer
- Two tenant representatives: Kofi (west side) and a member introduced only as "R." who asked not to be named in notes
- Housing coalition policy lead: Lotte
- Me, taking notes for the thesis interview record, with verbal consent to record and quote anonymously

Meeting held at the coalition's storefront office, Thursday evening, 6:30 to 8pm, as part of the community-perspective interviews feeding into the qualitative chapter. Purpose was to hear directly from renters about displacement pressure before I finalize the survey instrument, not to present findings — I said as much at the start and tried to stick to it throughout. Dana had reserved the back room so the conversation could stay off the main floor, which R. later said made a real difference in how comfortable she felt speaking.

## Agenda and Context

Dana opened with a short update on two active tenant organizing campaigns in buildings near the transit corridor I'm studying, both involving rent increases following recent renovations. She framed the meeting as a chance for the coalition to talk back to research rather than just be a data source for it, which I appreciated and want to hold myself to — several past interviewees have mentioned feeling extracted from rather than partnered with by prior researchers.

We spent the first twenty minutes on introductions and context-setting rather than jumping into questions, which felt slower than planned but was clearly the right call given the room. Kofi mentioned upfront that he'd be careful about details since his building's landlord has retaliated against tenants who spoke to press before, naming a specific incident from last year where a local news segment led to a lease non-renewal for the tenant quoted in it. R. asked several clarifying questions about how the interview notes would be used, what anonymization actually means in practice, and whether the coalition would see a draft before anything goes into the thesis — reasonable questions I should have anticipated better and built into my consent language from the start rather than answering somewhat improvised on the spot. Dana also asked whether the coalition would be credited or cited in the final thesis, and I said yes, as an organization, with individual quotes kept anonymous unless someone explicitly wants otherwise.

## Discussion Highlights

Kofi described a rent increase of just over 30% following a renovation that added little his household actually wanted — new lobby flooring, a package room — while a persistent leak in his unit went unaddressed for months. He connected this directly to the transit corridor: the renovation was explicitly marketed around proximity to the new station, and he believes the improvements were aimed at attracting new tenants willing to pay more rather than retaining existing ones. This matches a pattern Lotte said the coalition has tracked across four buildings near the same corridor over the past two years, which she offered to share as aggregated case summaries rather than named files.

> [!tip] Quote worth keeping
> "Nobody asks us if we want the amenities. They ask us if we can still afford to live here after they add them." — R., paraphrased with permission for the notes, not a direct recording quote.

R. spoke more cautiously but described a similar pattern in a different building, adding that informal pressure — non-renewal threats, slow maintenance response — often precedes any formal rent increase, which lines up with the informal-eviction gap I flagged in the [[165-reading-notes-eviction-economy]] critique. She also mentioned that her landlord stopped responding to a mold complaint entirely after she asked, in an earlier conversation, about her lease renewal terms, which she read as a deliberate signal rather than ordinary neglect. Lotte pushed back gently on my framing at one point, noting that "displacement risk" as a survey category can flatten experiences that are really about dignity and control, not just affordability math, and suggested the survey's open-ended section needs to make more room for that distinction than my current draft does. She offered an example question from a past coalition survey that asked residents to describe a moment they felt least in control of their housing, which struck me as a genuinely better prompt than anything I'd drafted so far.

## Implications for Fieldwork

This conversation is reshaping two parts of the project. First, the survey's open-ended prompts in [[162-survey-design-project]] need revision — Lotte's point about dignity versus pure affordability is well taken, and I want at least one question that doesn't assume displacement risk is purely financial. Second, Kofi's offer to help recruit two households for cognitive interview pretesting is a real opening I should follow up on within the week, since coalition-vouched recruitment likely solves the trust problem Voss's conference talk raised about door-to-door strangers.

There's also a methodological tension I need to sit with rather than resolve tonight: the coalition members I'm talking to are, almost by definition, the more organized and vocal tenants, which may not represent households who are isolated, newly arrived, or otherwise disconnected from tenant organizing. I don't think this invalidates the interviews, but it means I can't treat coalition perspective as a stand-in for the full renter population without saying so explicitly in the methods chapter. Dana herself raised something close to this concern unprompted, saying the coalition mostly hears from tenants who already have some capacity to organize, and that the households under the most acute pressure are often the hardest to reach precisely because organizing takes time and energy they don't have. That observation on its own might be worth a direct citation in the limitations section, attributed to her by name if she consents when I follow up.
