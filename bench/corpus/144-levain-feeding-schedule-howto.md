A levain, in the sense most bread recipes mean it, is a small offshoot build made specifically for one bake rather than the ongoing mother starter itself. Keeping the two separate has solved more of my timing headaches than any other single change I've made to how I regularly bake, by a wide margin.

## Building a levain from the mother starter

The core idea is simple: rather than using your maintained starter directly in a dough, you take a small amount of it and build a fresh, larger batch specifically timed to peak right when you plan to mix. This matters because your ongoing starter, the one you feed regularly to keep alive, isn't necessarily on the schedule your actual bake needs; if you feed it in the morning and want to mix dough at 7pm, a once-daily-fed starter may have already peaked and started declining hours before you're ready, giving you a weaker rise than a properly timed levain would.

Start by deciding your target mix time, then work backward. Most levain builds take four to eight hours to peak at room temperature, depending on ratio, temperature, and how active your mother starter currently is. A common ratio is 1:5:5, one part mother starter to five parts flour to five parts water by weight, which gives a fairly quick build, often peaking in four to six hours at a warm room temperature around 75F. A stiffer or cooler build, closer to 1:3:3, takes longer and gives you more scheduling flexibility if your day is unpredictable and you can't commit to a precise mix time in advance.

Feed the levain from a small, healthy portion of your mother starter, ideally taken shortly after the mother's own feeding when it's active but not yet at peak itself. Combine it with fresh flour and water at your chosen ratio in a clean jar, mark the current level with a rubber band the same way you would for a mother starter, and set it somewhere with a temperature you can reasonably predict, since ambient swings are the single biggest source of unpredictable timing in this whole process.

Watch for peak rather than trusting the clock alone, especially the first few times you build a levain in a new season or a new kitchen, since temperature swings between rooms and between seasons genuinely shift timing more than most guides account for. Peak looks like a dome that's just started to flatten slightly at the very center, still domed overall but with the first hint of collapse beginning, paired with a passing float test and a pleasant, active smell rather than anything sharp or overly acidic. Using a levain past peak gives a weaker, less lively dough in the same way an overripe mother starter does; using it before peak means the yeast population hasn't fully built up yet and your bulk fermentation will simply take longer to compensate, which isn't fatal but does throw off any timeline you've planned around a specific mix time.

One habit that's helped enormously: building the levain the night before for a morning mix, using the cooler, stiffer ratio to slow things down enough that it holds near peak for a wider window rather than needing precise middle-of-the-night timing. This trades a bit of the levain's peak liveliness for a much more forgiving schedule, which for a home baker fitting bread around a normal work day is almost always the better trade. I only reach for same-day, fast-build levains now when I know I'll actually be home and free to mix at the exact planned time, reserving the overnight slow-build approach for every other more typical, less predictable day.

Discard the levain jar after each bake rather than trying to maintain it long-term; that's what the mother starter is for. Treating the levain as genuinely disposable, rather than something to keep feeding indefinitely, removed a surprising amount of mental overhead from my weekly baking routine once I actually let myself do it instead of feeling wasteful about tossing active culture down the drain.

## A quick reference

Fast build (1:5:5, warm room): peaks in four to six hours, best for a same-day mix you can commit to precisely. Slow build (1:3:3, cooler spot): peaks in eight to twelve hours, better for an overnight schedule with a wider forgiving window before the actual morning mix. When in doubt, especially in an unfamiliar kitchen or an unpredictable day ahead, default to the slower build and simply plan the rest of your schedule around it instead.
