Woke up at 5am again, mind already running the numbers before coffee. This has been happening a lot since we crossed the halfway point on the [[220-barbell-investing-strategy]] plan last month, and I keep expecting the milestone to feel more solid than it does. Instead I lie there running scenarios: what if the market drops 30% the year before we pull the trigger, what if healthcare costs come in higher than the estimate in [[240-what-early-retirement-actually-costs]], what if one of us needs a procedure that isn't fully covered. None of these are new worries, but they hit differently at 5am than they do in a spreadsheet at noon. Priyam mentioned something similar last week when we grabbed coffee, said the closer she got to her own number, the more she second-guessed whether the number was even right in the first place, whether she'd built in enough margin for the life she actually wants to live rather than the bare-bones version she modeled two years ago when the whole plan started as a reaction to a bad quarter at work. I think that's the real thing underneath the doubt: it's not really about the math, the math has been checked and rechecked, it's about whether the life on the other side of the number is one I actually want, or whether I've just gotten so used to optimizing that I wouldn't know what to do with unstructured time. That question doesn't have a spreadsheet answer, which is probably why it shows up at 5am instead of at my desk.

## Morning check-in

By the time I actually got up, made coffee, and sat down with the account balances open, most of the anxiety had drained out of it the way it usually does. The portfolio is up 4.2% year to date, contributions are on schedule, and the FI number itself hasn't moved in three months, which is its own kind of reassurance even if it doesn't feel like progress in the moment. I went back through the assumptions one more time: 3.8% real withdrawal rate, healthcare estimated at $1,400/month for two people pre-Medicare, a ten percent buffer on top of bare expenses for the inevitable stuff that doesn't show up in a monthly budget. Property tax increases, a roof eventually, a car replacement every eight to ten years. Written out like that it looks reasonable, even conservative, but reasonable on paper and reassuring in the body are two different things, and I don't think I've fully closed that gap yet. Talked to Dana about it over breakfast, mostly just saying the worry out loud rather than looking for a fix, and that helped more than another round of scenario modeling would have. Sometimes the plan doesn't need adjusting, the nervous system just needs a minute to catch up to the spreadsheet. I noticed I felt calmer once I said the specific fear out loud instead of letting it stay a vague background hum, which is probably worth remembering the next time this happens, because it will happen again before we're actually done with this. Dana pointed out, gently, that I've had a version of this same morning at least four times since we started tracking toward the number seriously, and every time the resolution has been the same: the plan was fine, the numbers were fine, the only thing that needed attention was the feeling itself, not a spreadsheet cell. I want to trust that pattern more than I currently do, and writing it down here is partly an attempt to build that trust over time, one entry at a time, rather than relearning the same lesson from scratch every few months.

## Deciding not to act

Decided not to touch anything in the plan today. The instinct when anxious is to do something, adjust an allocation, move a number, add a buffer that wasn't there yesterday, but none of that would address the actual source of the unease, which is uncertainty about the future rather than a flaw in the current plan. Instead I wrote this down, which is its own kind of action, and told myself I'd revisit the whole thing calmly at the next quarterly review in April rather than making changes from a place of half-awake dread. That feels like the right call, even if it's the less satisfying one in the moment, and I want to remember that distinction the next time 5am rolls around uninvited. There's a pattern I keep noticing where anxiety disguises itself as diligence, where "just double-checking the numbers one more time" is really just a way of feeling like I'm doing something about a feeling that doesn't actually respond to more spreadsheet work. Naming that pattern here feels more useful than another pass through the withdrawal-rate assumptions, checked probably a dozen times this year alone.

## What would actually help

Talking to someone who's already retired early instead of just reading about it would help more than another spreadsheet pass. Priyam's two years ahead of us on this path and clearly still working through her own version of this doubt, so misery loves company isn't quite it, but comparing notes with someone living the transition rather than just planning for it feels like it would ground the abstract fear in something more concrete and specific. I should ask her how she structured her first six months, whether she kept any kind of routine from her old job on purpose, and whether the 5am spiral ever fully went away or just got quieter with time and repetition. Might message her this week instead of letting it sit as a vague someday-conversation on my mental list.
