# Storm Mechanic Journal

I keep coming back to the storm track and I think I've finally found the version I'm willing to commit to for the print proof. For months the storm has been a single die roll each round: roll, move the storm marker that many spaces, done. It was easy to teach, but every playtest group said the same thing, that the storm felt like weather rather than a threat, something that happened to them instead of something they were racing against. Kofi pointed out back in March that a random roll each round meant players couldn't plan more than one turn ahead, which undercuts the whole "coastal warden" fantasy where you're supposed to be reading the sky and making calls. I sat with that complaint for a long time because the fix that seemed obvious, a fixed storm schedule printed on a card, felt like it would kill replayability stone dead. Nobody wants to play the same storm twice.

The version I'm testing now splits the difference: a face-down storm deck of twelve cards, drawn one per round, but each card shows two numbers instead of one, a "minimum advance" and a "maximum advance," and the acting warden that round chooses which value applies by spending or saving a wind token. It keeps the deck from being fully random since both values are fixed and visible once drawn, but it also hands players a real decision instead of a die roll they can't influence. I like that it ties the storm to the wind-token economy Dana and I sketched for the warden roles back in January, so it's not a bolted-on subsystem. #tidewatch #design

## Playtest reaction

Ran this with Tomasz and two of his friends on Thursday night, first time anyone outside the four of us has seen the deck version. The reaction was immediately more engaged than any storm-track session I've run before; Tomasz actually said out loud "wait, we can slow it down if we save tokens," which is exactly the moment of realization I was hoping to trigger, and it happened without me explaining the subsystem beyond the two rulebook sentences on the reference card. One of his friends, playing the Watch role, spent her first three wind tokens hoarding rather than spending, which let the storm crawl at minimum pace for four rounds while the group repositioned the evacuation boats, and then the storm caught up hard in round eight when the deck ran low on cheap cards and she'd run out of tokens to soften it. That swing, slow-then-fast, is closer to what a real storm front does than the flat average pace the die roll produced, and it's the first time a playtest group has described the ending as "tense" rather than "long," which felt like real validation after months of the same lukewarm feedback. Kofi still wants to see it survive a four-player game where all four wardens are trying to hoard tokens simultaneously, since the deck assumes at least some spending happens, and if nobody spends, the storm might crawl the whole game and remove the threat entirely rather than delaying it, which would be a worse failure mode than the flat die roll it's replacing. That's the test for next week: deliberately try to break it by refusing to spend a single token before round six and see whether the deck design has a built-in pressure valve or whether I need to add one, maybe a card that forces a minimum advance regardless of tokens saved, sitting somewhere in the back third of the deck as a hard stop, printed with a distinct border so the group knows it's coming without knowing exactly when it will land.


## Open questions

A few things I still don't have good answers for. First, does the twelve-card deck need to reshuffle if the game runs long, or should running out of storm cards be an automatic loss condition, which would be thematically fitting but might feel cheap to a group that's otherwise winning and close to the evacuation goal. Second, I haven't tested this with only two players, where the wind-token economy might behave completely differently since there's no one else to coordinate hoarding with, and the deck's pacing was tuned against four-player token generation. Third, Lotte asked whether the storm deck should be a Kickstarter stretch-goal upgrade to fancier cardstock, which is a marketing question dressed up as a design one, but not one I can answer alone; I'll leave that with her for [[292-tidewatch-kickstarter-prep]] and focus on the two-player test this weekend instead. I also want to ask Priyam whether the stretch-goal cardstock question changes anything about the art pass timeline, since that's a real dependency between marketing and production I hadn't considered until Lotte raised it.


## Next steps

Run the deliberate-hoarding test with the full four-player group before Thursday, specifically trying to stall the storm past round eight to see if it breaks the pacing entirely or just delays the tension curve by a round or two. Draft a two-player variant of the wind-token rule for testing this weekend, probably giving the sole active warden two tokens per round instead of one to compensate for the missing coordination between roles. Write up whatever happens for [[286-tidewatch-rulebook-review-meeting]] so Kofi has real numbers instead of my gut feeling going into that meeting, and bring both sets of results, two-player and four-player, so we can compare pacing side by side and decide, once and for all, whether the two-player variant needs its own token count or can just reuse the four-player numbers.
