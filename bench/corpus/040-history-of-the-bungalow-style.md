# A Short History of the Bungalow, and Why It Landed on This Street

I started digging into the history of the bungalow style partly out of guilt, after realizing halfway through the kitchen renovation that I'd spent months making decisions about a house whose lineage I couldn't explain to anyone who asked. The word comes from "bangla," a Hindi and Bengali term for a low, thatched house typical of rural Bengal, adapted by British colonial administrators for officials' housing across India in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. That colonial version was single-story, verandah-wrapped, built for a hot climate, only loosely mapping onto what got built on streets like ours. The style crossed to Britain and then North America in the late 1800s, but it was California, in the early 1900s, where the bungalow as most people now picture it took shape, driven by architects like the Greene brothers, whose Arts and Crafts sensibility fused with the colonial template into something new: low roofs, deep eaves, exposed rafter tails, and an honesty about structure that pushed against the ornamentation of the Victorians it was replacing.

By the 1920s the bungalow had become, more than anything else, an affordable and mass-producible house type, spread nationwide through pattern books and mail-order kit homes that let a family order house plans, or in some cases entire pre-cut lumber packages, straight from a catalog. That democratization is a big part of why bungalows cluster so densely in certain neighborhoods even now; they were, for a couple of decades, simply the default choice for a modest, well-built family home. Our own house dates from 1962, which puts it at the tail end of the style's popularity, well past the classic Arts and Crafts boom of the 1910s and 1920s, into a period when "bungalow" had loosened into a generic term for any modest one-story house, regardless of how closely it hewed to the Greene brothers' original principles.

> [!note] What actually makes ours a bungalow
> Low-pitched roof, one and a half stories with a partial attic dormer, a covered front porch under the main roofline, and simple, honest materials throughout: brick, wood siding, no applied ornament pretending to be something it isn't.
> By 1962, the label mostly signaled "modest and efficient" rather than any specific movement.

## Why the Style Fits This Renovation

Understanding this history has actually shaped some of the choices we've made in the kitchen and workshop. The Arts and Crafts emphasis on honest materials pushed us toward the zellige tile and matte cabinet finishes discussed in [[032-kitchen-renovation-plan]] rather than anything more polished or applied-looking, and it's part of why we kept the original window placement rather than resizing for a more modern look that would have looked out of place against a roofline this age. The workshop, too, borrows from the bungalow ethos more than I initially realized: a simple, low-pitched structure with exposed rafters inside, built to be functional first, rather than the more decorative sheds you sometimes see marketed as "she-sheds" or backyard studios.

## What Got Lost Along the Way

Not every 1960s bungalow retained its original character, and ours certainly didn't; the 1970s kitchen we just tore out was a clear example of later owners chasing whatever was fashionable at the time rather than honoring the original design intent, laminate counters and all. Aluminum sliding windows, dropped acoustic ceilings, and wall-to-wall shag carpet all show up in old photos Lotte found in the county archive of our address from a 1978 real estate listing, all choices that would have felt current then and now just read as a layer to strip back toward whatever's underneath, a pattern that probably repeats every twenty or thirty years regardless of which decade happens to be doing the stripping back. It's a useful reminder, mid-renovation, that we're not the first people to make choices in this house that felt permanent at the time and turned out not to be, and it's tempered some of my own certainty about the sage cabinets and brass hardware feeling timeless. They might just be our decade's version of the shag carpet, and that's fine.

## Reading the Whole Street Differently Now

Once you start noticing bungalow details, it's hard to stop, and it's changed how I walk around the neighborhood entirely. Half the houses on our street share the same low roof pitch and deep eaves, though almost none of them still have their original windows, and most have been resided at least once in sixty-some years, vinyl over the original wood in more cases than I'd like to count. There's a pale yellow one two doors down that clearly still has its original porch columns, tapered and resting on brick piers exactly like ours did before a previous owner squared them off with plain four-by-fours sometime in the eighties, visible in the same archive photos Lotte pulled for our own house. I've started, half-jokingly, cataloging which houses on the block have kept which original features, a project Priyam finds mildly ridiculous but has started contributing to anyway, spotting a matching porch rail detail on a house three streets over during a completely unrelated walk last weekend. It's turned a fairly ordinary evening walk into something closer to a scavenger hunt, and I suspect it's made me a more sympathetic, and probably more opinionated, neighbor than I was before any of this renovation started, for better or worse depending on who you ask about it. I've even started drafting, mostly for my own amusement, a short walking-tour note of the block's remaining original details, in case anyone else on the street ever wants to compare notes over a fence.

## A Small Correction to My Own Assumptions

I'd always assumed "bungalow" specifically meant a small house, but the history shows the label got applied to some genuinely large homes too, especially in wealthier early-1900s neighborhoods, once it became more a stylistic marker than a size category.
