# How to Run a Tidewatch Playtest

This guide covers running an external Tidewatch session end to end, from setup through debrief, and it's written for anyone on the team stepping in to facilitate, not just Kofi. It assumes you already know the current rules draft and have the latest component set from [[277-tidewatch-component-list]] packed and ready to go before players arrive, with nothing missing from the box.

## Running the Session

Arrive early enough to set up the board and sort components before players show up; a rushed setup in front of a new group makes the game look more complicated than it is, and first impressions matter more than you'd think for a cooperative game where trust in the system is part of the experience. Lay out the island tiles first, then place the storm deck and tide markers where every seat can reach them without leaning across the table, since Tidewatch has enough shared components that awkward reach becomes a real friction point once the group is four players deep into a tense round.

Introduce the theme before the rules. Tell players they're lighthouse keepers and coastal wardens racing a storm to evacuate a chain of islands, and let that framing do some of the teaching work before you ever mention a token or a die. Kofi has found that groups who hear the theme first ask fewer "why does this rule exist" questions later, since the mechanics feel like consequences of the premise rather than arbitrary constraints.

Teach roles one at a time, starting with whichever role is simplest that session, and have each player read their own role card aloud rather than summarizing it for them; players retain their own role's exceptions better when they've said the words themselves, and it also surfaces confusing wording in the card text immediately, which is useful data even outside the playtest's main goal. Don't teach every edge case up front. Cover the core loop, then let edge cases come up naturally during play and answer them as they arise, noting each one down as you go rather than trying to anticipate everything in the teach.

Once play starts, resist the urge to jump in and correct suboptimal decisions. The whole point of an external playtest is watching what a group does without the designer's steering hand, so let a bad token-spending choice play out even if you can see it's going to hurt them two rounds later; the way a mistake compounds tells you more about the pacing curve than a clean, optimal playthrough would. Take notes throughout rather than relying on memory afterward. A simple format works fine: round number, what happened, and a one-line read on whether it felt right, wrong, or unclear to the table. Kofi's running log for this has been a plain text file with a timestamp per entry, nothing fancier, and it's been enough to catch every major balance issue so far.

Watch specifically for moments of silence or stalling, where a player isn't sure what to do next; those moments are almost always a sign the rulebook or the reference card failed to communicate something, not that the player is slow. Write down exactly what they were confused about and what question they eventually asked, since the gap between those two things is usually where the fix needs to go. Also watch for the opposite problem, a player who's clearly solved the puzzle and is quietly directing everyone else; that's the "captain problem" Dana has been trying to design out of the game since the earliest prototypes, and every instance of it is worth flagging even if the rest of the session went smoothly.

If the group finishes early or loses badly, resist the urge to explain away the loss as bad luck unless you've actually checked the storm deck draws against what a reasonable draw would look like. A loss that feels unfair because of a genuinely unlucky sequence is a different problem than a loss caused by a rule the group misunderstood, and conflating the two in your notes will send the next design iteration in the wrong direction. When in doubt, ask the table directly: did that feel unfair, or did it feel like we made a mistake somewhere? Their answer, even an uncertain one, is more useful than your own guess from across the table.

Keep sessions to roughly ninety minutes including teach time where possible. Longer sessions produce fatigue that shows up in your notes as false balance complaints, when really the group is just tired and less forgiving of ambiguity than an hour earlier.

## Debrief

Once the game ends, spend ten minutes talking through the session while it's fresh, before anyone packs up. Ask what felt tense, what felt slow, and whether the ending felt earned. Write their answers down verbatim where possible rather than paraphrasing; players' exact words often surface a rules gap Kofi would otherwise miss. Log results in the shared sheet the same day, referencing [[274-how-to-run-tidewatch-playtest|this guide]] for anyone new who joins the rotation of facilitators later this season.
