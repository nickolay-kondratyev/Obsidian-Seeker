# The Case for a Rush Premium That Actually Hurts to Charge

For most of the time I've run this studio, my rush fee has been a token gesture rather than a real reflection of cost: a flat twenty percent tacked onto the invoice whenever a client needed a turnaround under a week, more habit than calculation, something I added because other studios seemed to do it and because it felt vaguely wrong to charge the same rate for a scramble as for a normally scheduled shoot. After the five-week holiday stretch I wrote about in [[208-holiday-rush-journal]], seven bookings back to back with zero buffer days between them, I finally sat down and actually costed out what a rush booking does to the rest of my calendar rather than just eyeballing a round number that felt roughly fair. The honest answer surprised me: a single rush booking dropped into an already full week doesn't just cost the hours it takes, it costs the buffer day I'd normally protect around it, which means the real price of saying yes is closer to a day and a half of capacity than the few extra hours the client actually sees on the invoice. Once I ran the numbers that way, my old twenty percent flat fee looked almost arbitrary, disconnected from what a rush booking genuinely does to the weeks around it rather than just the booking itself. This article is my attempt to work out, in writing, what a rush premium should actually be doing, and why I think most photographers, myself included until recently, set it too low because we're pricing the visible inconvenience instead of the invisible one that shows up two days later when there's no slack left anywhere in the schedule to absorb the next thing that inevitably goes wrong. It's a narrow claim about pricing structure specifically, not an argument that studios should charge more across the board, and I want to be careful not to let one bad holiday stretch turn into an excuse for padding every invoice. The point isn't that rush jobs deserve punishment, it's that the fee should track the real cost a booking imposes, and a flat number can't do that consistently across a calendar that's sometimes wide open and sometimes already packed.

## Why the Old Number Felt Wrong

Twenty percent flat never distinguished between a rush booking dropped into an open week, which costs almost nothing extra, and one dropped into an already tight week, which costs real capacity elsewhere. Treating both identically meant I was underpricing the second case consistently, exactly the case that led directly into the burnout stretch from last winter, and the flat number gave me no honest signal about which situation I was actually in until the damage was already done further down the calendar. Worse, the flat fee actively discouraged me from checking the calendar carefully before quoting, since the number was going to be the same either way, so I'd quote from memory and only discover the real cost once the week was already committed and there was nothing left to do about it.

## What the Real Cost Looks Like

Working back through the holiday stretch booking by booking, the pattern was consistent: every rush booking that landed in an already-full week either ate a planned buffer day directly or pushed the next booking's prep time down to almost nothing, which is exactly the compounding effect that made the whole five-week stretch feel like one continuous shoot with no real seams between jobs. When I costed that out in hours rather than just vibes, a rush booking in a tight week was running closer to forty percent more total time cost than the invoice reflected, once lost buffer and compressed prep are counted honestly instead of ignored. A rush booking in an otherwise open week, by contrast, barely registered as extra cost at all beyond the faster editing turnaround itself, which told me the flat fee was punishing the wrong bookings and letting the genuinely expensive ones through underpriced the whole time. I went back through eleven months of the invoice tracker to check this held outside the holiday stretch too, and it did, consistently enough that December wasn't a one-time fluke.

## The Practical Version

Starting this quarter, rush pricing is now tiered by calendar context rather than flat: a rush booking landing in a week with an existing buffer day open costs a modest fifteen percent, basically covering the faster editing turnaround and nothing more, since it isn't actually displacing anything else on the calendar. A rush booking landing in a week that's already full, the case that actually hurt last December, now carries a thirty-five percent premium, priced explicitly as buying back the buffer day it displaces rather than as a vague inconvenience surcharge, which is the honest thing this fee should have always been doing. I've started telling returning clients this directly and in writing before they book, rather than letting them discover the tiered structure only when a quote comes back higher than they expected, since a rush fee that surprises people at invoice time reads as punitive even when the underlying logic is sound and defensible. Priyam and I also agreed the thirty-five percent tier only applies once we've actually checked the calendar together, not as a snap judgment made under pressure on a phone call, since a five-minute look at the shared calendar before quoting has already caught two cases this month where a slot looked full at a glance but genuinely wasn't once we checked properly. The tiered structure also gave me language for saying no more comfortably: if a rush request would require bumping an already-committed client rather than just tightening my own schedule, I now decline outright rather than quoting an even higher premium that was really just a polite way of hoping the client would say no themselves instead of me having to.

## Where I'm Still Unsure

I don't yet know if thirty-five percent is the right number or just a better guess than twenty, and I want at least two more full quarters of real bookings before I trust it as settled rather than provisional.
