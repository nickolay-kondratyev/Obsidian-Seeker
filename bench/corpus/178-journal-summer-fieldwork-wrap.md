August 29, 2026

Last day of summer fieldwork, technically. I still have two interviews to transcribe and a stack of curb-cut audit sheets to digitize, but the recruiting window closes today and I am done chasing respondents through the heat. Twelve weeks, forty-two survey completions, nineteen interviews, three neighborhoods walked block by block with a measuring wheel and a clipboard that is now held together with packing tape. I want to write this down honestly before the semester swallows the memory of it, because by October I know I'll only remember the clean version — the one where everything went according to the [[162-survey-design-project]] plan I wrote back in April. It mostly didn't, and that's fine, but I want the messy version on record too, for my own sake and for whoever reads this in five years wondering why the methods section hedges as much as it does. The last two weeks blurred together into a rotation of bus stops, waivers, and voice memos I'd forget to label until the third listen.

## What Worked

The intercept survey approach at the transit stops worked better than I expected once I stopped trying to be neutral about approaching people — smiling and saying "got two minutes, I'm a grad student not selling anything" got a much higher response rate than my more formal opener. Riding the bus with a laptop open and just observing boarding patterns for an hour at a time, three times a week, gave me a texture for the corridor that no dataset could. The interview protocol held up well too; only had to revise one question after the first three interviews felt stilted, and Dana's suggestion to open with "walk me through your morning commute" instead of a demographic question completely changed how relaxed people were. Partnering with the community center on the east side for interview space was the single best logistical decision of the summer — free, quiet, and people already trusted the building. I also got faster at transcription than I thought possible by week six, probably because I stopped fighting the software and just accepted a rougher first pass, cleaning it up on the second listen instead of trying to get it perfect in one go. Keeping a running fieldnote file open on my phone, separate from the formal interview log, turned out to be essential — half my best analytic hunches came from a two-line note jotted on the bus home, not from anything I wrote sitting at a desk later trying to reconstruct the mood of a conversation after the fact.

## What Didn't

Geocoding the curb-cut locations from hand-drawn field sketches was slower and messier than planned — I lost almost a full week to it in July reconciling sketch coordinates against the parcel map. Two scheduled interviews fell through entirely and I never found replacements in that demographic slice, which I'll have to flag honestly as a limitation rather than quietly papering over in the writeup. The recorder also died mid-interview once, and I'm still not fully confident in my handwritten backup notes from that session.

## Exhaustion and Gratitude

I am tired in a way that surprised me, given that fieldwork is supposedly the "easy" part before the writing grind starts. There's a specific fatigue to being "on" for every interview, calibrating tone, listening for the thing under the thing someone is saying, and then immediately switching to fieldnotes mode to capture it before it evaporates. Some days I did three interviews back to back and drove home unable to form a sentence. But I'm also more grateful than I expected to be. Grateful to the nineteen people who let a stranger with a recorder into an hour of their day for nothing but a coffee gift card and my thanks. Grateful to Kofi at the community center who kept unlocking the side room for me even on days I showed up late. Grateful to Lotte for reading three different consent-form drafts before IRB finally approved the one that stuck. And grateful, honestly, to past-me for front-loading so much of the logistics work in the spring, because summer-me had exactly zero bandwidth left for anything beyond showing up and listening. There were mornings on the bus, watching the same regulars board at the same stops, when I remembered why I wanted to do this kind of work in the first place, before it turned into a spreadsheet and a defense date. I don't want to lose that thread once I'm back to staring at a screen for twelve hours a day. I want to remember the woman at the east-side stop who told me she'd timed her whole morning around a bus that was cut two years ago and never came back, because that sentence is the reason this thesis exists, not the regression tables it will eventually produce. I also want to remember how much slower trust builds than a project timeline assumes it will — the interviews that went best were almost always the third or fourth conversation with someone, not the first, and no Gantt chart accounts for that.

## Looking Ahead

September is transcription and coding, full stop — no more data collection, no more "just one more interview." October is when the writing has to start in earnest for a full draft to Dr. Voss by winter break. I already feel the pull to keep collecting instead of analyzing, but I know that instinct is avoidance in disguise.
