Shaping and scoring are the two steps that took me the longest to get comfortable with, well after the starter maintenance and hydration math had become second nature. This is the routine I finally settled on after enough flat, torn, or oddly-blooming loaves to force real attention on technique rather than just following instructions half-mindedly.

## The full routine

Start shaping only once bulk fermentation is actually done, not on a timer — the dough should feel airy and slightly jiggly when you nudge the bowl, with visible bubbles at the surface and a noticeable increase in volume, usually somewhere between 50 and 75 percent depending on temperature and the particular starter's strength. Turn the dough out onto a lightly floured surface, handling it as little as possible at this stage since every extra touch degasses some of the structure you've spent hours building. Do a gentle preshape first: fold the edges toward the center to form a loose round, then let it rest uncovered for fifteen to twenty minutes, which relaxes the gluten enough that final shaping won't fight the dough's own tension.

For the final shape, flip the rested round so the smooth side faces down, then work the edges inward and underneath in a series of folds, building surface tension as you go rather than trying to create it in one motion. I fold from the far edge toward myself, rotate a quarter turn, fold again, and repeat until the dough feels taut across the top without tearing, usually four to six folds depending on how much the preshape already relaxed things. The goal is a smooth, tight skin that will hold its shape during the final proof and give the loaf structure to expand upward rather than spreading outward in the oven. Too little tension and the loaf spreads flat; too much and the surface tears before it even reaches the basket, so this is genuinely a feel-based skill that improved for me only through repetition, not through reading more descriptions of what tension should feel like.

Once shaped, transfer seam-side up into a well-floured banneton or a bowl lined with a floured towel, and let it do its final proof either at room temperature for two to four hours or in the fridge overnight for a slower, more flavorful result and a firmer dough that's easier to score cleanly the next morning. Cold dough straight from the fridge holds a blade line far better than warm dough, which tends to drag and distort under the lame, so the overnight fridge proof isn't just about flavor development, it's a real practical advantage for anyone still building scoring confidence.

Scoring itself starts with flipping the proofed dough out of its banneton onto parchment, seam-side down now, smooth side up and facing the oven. Use a sharp blade, ideally a proper bread lame rather than a kitchen knife, held at a shallow angle, closer to 45 degrees than straight down, which creates the shelf that lets the crust peel back and bloom dramatically during oven spring rather than just splitting awkwardly wherever the dough finds the weakest point on its own. One confident, continuous stroke works better than several hesitant ones; hesitation tends to produce a ragged, uneven cut that doesn't bloom cleanly even if the depth is technically correct. Depth matters too — too shallow and the cut won't guide the expansion at all, too deep and you risk deflating the careful tension built during shaping, so somewhere around a quarter inch has worked best for me across most loaf sizes and hydrations I regularly bake.

I've also started adding a couple of smaller decorative cuts alongside the main score on loaves meant for sharing, partly for appearance and partly because a secondary release point seems to reduce the chance of the crust tearing unpredictably somewhere else on the loaf during the most vigorous part of oven spring. It's a small thing, more art than necessity, but it's become part of the routine I actually enjoy now instead of dreading, which says something about how much shaping and scoring have gone from the most stressful part of baking to genuinely one of the most satisfying.

## Common failures

A loaf that spreads flat instead of rising up usually means underdeveloped tension during shaping, not a weak starter as I originally assumed for months. A score that seals shut instantly rather than blooming usually means the blade angle was too vertical rather than shallow. And a loaf that tears somewhere other than the score line almost always traces back to skipped or rushed final tension-building during shaping itself, something worth checking before blaming the flour or the oven.
