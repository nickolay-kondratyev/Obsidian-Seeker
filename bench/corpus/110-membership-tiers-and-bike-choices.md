# Membership Tiers and Bike Choices

A question that comes up constantly from prospective members, usually phrased something like 'what kind of bike do I need to join,' and the honest answer is more complicated than most people want at the point of asking. The club doesn't enforce a bike requirement, but the practical reality is that different ride categories suit different bikes, and turning up on the wrong equipment for a given ride can mean a genuinely miserable few hours rather than a minor inconvenience. This piece tries to lay out, honestly and without any sales pitch for one type of bike over another, what actually works for which kind of riding the club does, since a hybrid perfectly suited to the weekly social ride will struggle badly on the gravel loop, and a lightweight road bike for time trials isn't the right tool for a loaded multi-day tour. None of this is about gatekeeping; the point of writing it down is the opposite, helping new members avoid a mismatch between bike and route rather than any lack of fitness or effort on their part. Membership itself has never been tiered by bike type and never will be; anyone can turn up on anything roadworthy to the weekly social ride, which remains deliberately the most accessible fixture on the calendar. What follows is a rough guide rather than a rulebook, and plenty of members happily own more than one bike for exactly this reason, switching depending on which ride they're doing that weekend. Budget is obviously a real constraint for a lot of people too, and the club has never wanted to be the kind of place where the price of admission is implicitly a decent bike; several long-standing members started on secondhand hybrids bought for under a hundred pounds and only upgraded years later once they'd decided cycling was something they wanted to invest more seriously in, rather than the other way around. Priyam in particular likes to point out that his first season was spent on a bike he now describes, fondly, as an absolute liability, and it didn't stop him riding every single Saturday regardless.

## The Main Categories

Roughly speaking, club riding falls into four categories: the weekly social ride, faster paced club rides, gravel and mixed-terrain routes like the loop described in [[092-gravel-loop-mapping-project]], and multi-day touring. Each has a rough bike profile that works best, though there's plenty of overlap and plenty of members who make an imperfect bike work through sheer stubbornness and good humour rather than swapping equipment every season. The categories aren't official club terminology either, just a useful shorthand that's evolved informally over the years through conversations exactly like the ones that prompted writing this up in the first place, after yet another new member turned up to the gravel loop on 23mm road tyres and had a genuinely rough afternoon out on the rail bed section.

## A Rough Guide

| Ride type | Typical bike | Tyre width | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly social | Hybrid or road | 28-38mm | Comfort matters more than speed |
| Faster club rides | Road bike | 25-28mm | Lighter, stiffer frames help on climbs |
| Gravel loop | Gravel bike | 35-45mm | Wider tyres handle the rail bed section |
| Multi-day touring | Touring or gravel | 32-40mm | Needs rack and pannier mounts |
| Hill climb event | Road bike | 23-28mm | Low weight matters most here |

This table is deliberately rough; plenty of riders on 32mm road tyres manage the gravel loop fine in dry conditions, and plenty of gravel bikes keep up comfortably on the faster club rides too. Treat it as a starting point for conversation rather than a strict requirement, and if you're genuinely unsure which row describes your situation best, just ask in the group chat before a ride rather than guessing and turning up underprepared for the terrain or the pace.

## What Actually Matters More Than the Bike

Honestly, fit and maintenance matter more than bike category for most new members, and this point gets lost in conversations that focus too narrowly on frame material or tyre width. A well-fitted hybrid with properly inflated tyres and a clean, lubricated chain will outperform a poorly maintained road bike on almost every ride the club runs, and several of our strongest regular riders are on bikes that would raise eyebrows at a specialist shop but are set up and maintained meticulously. New members sometimes arrive worried that their bike isn't good enough, when the actual limiting factor turns out to be tyre pressure fifteen psi below where it should be, or a chain that hasn't seen lubricant since purchase. The maintenance clinic covers exactly this, and it's genuinely the single best investment a new member can make before worrying about whether to upgrade equipment at all. That said, there are real limits: a heavy hybrid with knobbly tyres will always struggle on a fast club ride regardless of how well maintained it is, and a lightweight road bike will always flounder on loose gravel no matter how skilled the rider, so the guide above still has real practical value once the basics of fit and maintenance are sorted out properly. The maintenance clinic itself is free for members and runs monthly, and honestly it's worth attending even for experienced riders, since the volunteers running it have collectively fixed more strange problems than most bike shops see in a year.

## Borrowing Before Buying

Before buying anything new, ask in the group chat first. Several members have spare bikes gathering dust and are happy to lend one for a trial ride before any big purchase decision gets made.
