Spent most of tonight fighting the borrow checker on the todo app write-back path, see [[062-cli-todo-app-project]], and I want to write down what actually happened before I forget the sequence of wrong turns, because I know from past languages that the second time I hit this exact error I'll want a record of how I reasoned through it rather than just the final fix. The bug: when I call `done` on a task, the in-memory vector updates fine, but writing it back to disk sometimes drops the last line entirely, and only sometimes, which made it maddening to reproduce reliably at first. I assumed it was a logic bug in my loop, so I spent almost an hour adding print statements before I even considered that the borrow checker's complaints earlier in the day might be related to the actual bug rather than just friction I'd already worked around with a clone. That turned out to be exactly backwards, and the two hours I spent chasing print statements before going back to first principles felt wasteful in the moment but taught me more about actually reading compiler output than any tutorial has so far, mostly because the mistakes were mine and the stakes were mine too. I even considered giving up for the night and picking it back up tomorrow with fresh eyes, but stubbornness won out around eight thirty, and honestly I'm glad it did because the actual fix turned out to be small enough that sleeping on it might have made me feel like I'd wasted the whole evening for nothing, when really the debugging itself was the point.

## Finding the actual bug

Around nine, I finally sat down and reread the function from scratch instead of patching around the symptom, which is advice I give other people constantly and apparently need to hear myself say out loud sometimes. I was joining lines with `\n` and writing the joined string, but the file writer used `write!` instead of `writeln!` at one point, which meant no trailing newline, and on some filesystems or some read passes that translated into effectively dropping the last record depending on how the reader split the content back apart afterward. It's such a small thing, one macro versus another, but it took real effort to find because I kept assuming the bug lived in the parsing logic where I'd already spent the most time defending my mental model of the code, not in the far more boring writing logic that I'd basically stopped looking at once it compiled the first time without errors. Lesson: when a bug seems to appear only sometimes, check the boundary conditions of whatever writes the data before assuming the reading side is wrong, because reading is usually where I look first out of habit and that habit isn't always earning its keep the way I assumed it would. Once I switched the macro I reran the full test suite three times in a row just to be sure it wasn't a flaky pass, and each run came back clean, which felt disproportionately satisfying for what was ultimately a one-character fix hiding behind two hours of misdirected debugging. I also added a regression test that specifically writes an odd number of tasks and reads them back, since the bug only ever showed up with certain line counts and I want future-me to catch this immediately rather than rediscovering it from scratch during some later refactor. Writing that test took maybe five minutes, which made the earlier detour feel even more avoidable in hindsight, though I suppose that's how debugging always feels once the answer is obvious and sitting right there in front of you.

## The earlier borrow checker fight

The borrow checker fights earlier today were a separate but related story. I wanted a function that takes `&mut Vec<Task>` and returns a `&Task` pointing into it, which the compiler correctly refused, and I spent a long time being annoyed before actually understanding why rather than just working around it with more clones. Dana explained it over a quick call: you can't hold an immutable borrow into a collection while also holding the mutable borrow needed to modify it, because the compiler can't prove the reference stays valid across a mutation that might reallocate the whole backing buffer underneath you. Once she framed it as "the compiler is protecting you from a use-after-free that would be trivial in C," it clicked in a way the book's phrasing hadn't quite managed for me on the first read, maybe because the book stays abstract and this was a bug I'd actually caused myself an hour earlier. I wrote a tiny standalone example afterward, stripped of the todo app context, just to make sure the lesson generalized and wasn't an artifact of my struct layout, and it did generalize cleanly.

## What I'm taking away

Two lessons stack neatly on top of each other tonight. First, ownership rules aren't arbitrary bureaucracy, they're preventing exactly the class of bug I used to chase for hours in C during my systems class years ago, and the compiler catching it upfront instead of at 2am in production is a trade I'll take every time even when it's frustrating in the moment. Second, when debugging feels stuck, going back to first principles and rereading the whole function beats incrementally patching symptoms, every single time, even though it feels slower in the moment when I just want the red squiggly to go away. I want to remember that the next time I'm three hours deep in print-statement debugging instead of just rereading the function like an adult who's done this before.
