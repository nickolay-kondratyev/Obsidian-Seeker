Marshal training and event-day briefing meeting, held two weeks before the spring century to get every volunteer up to speed on the route, radio procedure, and what to do in an emergency before the actual event day, rather than explaining everything on the morning itself when people are already nervous and time is short. This is now a standing fixture before any event needing more than six marshals, agreed at the annual volunteer coordination meeting earlier in the year. Present: Tomasz as volunteer coordinator, all fourteen marshals for the spring century, and Priyam covering the safety and emergency procedure section specifically. Held in the clubhouse on a Thursday evening, ran just under two hours including a practical radio exercise outside.

## Route Walkthrough

Tomasz went through the full route junction by junction, showing each marshal exactly where they'd be stationed on a printed map with numbered positions matching the GPX file, so there was no ambiguity about who covers what on the day itself. Particular attention went to the mill lane junction, still flagged with the sightline warning from the route safety review, where the assigned marshal was told explicitly to stand well back from the hedge corner and use a whistle rather than just a raised hand, since visibility there is poor enough that riders might not see a hand signal in time to react properly. Each marshal received a laminated card with their specific position, the nearest road name for emergency services, and a phone number to call if their assigned radio fails, since radio range past the reservoir has been unreliable in past events and a backup plan matters more there than anywhere else on the course. Questions came mostly from newer marshals about what counts as an emergency worth stopping the event for versus something to simply note and let organisers know about afterward, which Tomasz answered with specific examples rather than abstract guidance. One marshal asked what to do if a rider refuses to stop despite a clear safety concern; Tomasz explained marshals should never physically intervene, just note the details and radio it in. Another asked about toilet breaks during a four-hour shift, prompting a clarification that a buddy system between neighbouring positions covers exactly this.

## Radio Procedure

The club uses a shared radio channel for the day, with Tomasz as base station coordinating from the clubhouse and marshals checking in at set intervals rather than constant chatter that would make the channel unusable during an actual emergency. Priyam walked through the specific phrasing marshals should use: position number, status, and a brief description, kept short and clear rather than a long explanation that ties up the channel when someone else might urgently need it. A practical exercise followed outside in the car park, with marshals spread out at simulated positions and practising check-ins on the actual radios they'll use on the day, since a briefing on paper doesn't reveal the same problems as physically handling the equipment: two radios turned out to have weak batteries that would have failed partway through the event, caught only because of this practical run rather than assumed to be fine based on the briefing alone. Tomasz will swap those units before Saturday. The exercise also surfaced a genuine gap in coverage near the second rest stop, where the reservoir's terrain blocks signal more than expected; Priyam will position a spare marshal with a mobile phone as backup there specifically, treating radio silence in that zone as expected rather than alarming if it happens on the day itself. The exercise ran about twenty minutes, cycling every marshal through a full check-in so each person got comfortable with the phrasing and button timing on their specific radio, since a few older units have a slightly delayed transmit that catches people out under pressure. Tomasz timed a full roll call at just under four minutes, setting a reasonable expectation for how often a check-in is practical without eating into attention. By the end, even newer marshals who'd seemed unsure were handling their radios confidently, which Priyam noted was exactly the point of a practical session rather than just talking through the procedure verbally. Kofi, marshalling for the first time after mostly working the finish table, said the hands-on practice made him feel considerably less nervous than he had walking in.

## Emergency Procedure

Priyam covered the emergency procedure in detail: any marshal witnessing a serious incident calls emergency services directly first, then radios Tomasz with the position number and nature of the incident, rather than radioing first and losing time before getting professional help moving. The sag wagon, driven by Dana this year, will carry a full first-aid kit, spare wheels, and space for two riders needing to abandon, and can reach any point on the course within fifteen minutes based on this morning's timed test drive around the full loop. Marshals were reminded that their job in a genuine emergency is managing traffic and keeping other riders safe around the incident, not attempting first aid beyond very basic care unless they happen to hold a relevant qualification, since untrained intervention can sometimes do more harm than simply waiting the few minutes for the sag wagon or emergency services to arrive properly equipped and trained. A minor but important point Priyam stressed twice: every marshal should know the exact nearest postcode or landmark for their position before the day, not look it up under pressure, since precious minutes have been lost in the past fumbling for a location description while genuinely worried on the phone with emergency services. To make this concrete, each marshal wrote their nearest landmark on their laminated card by hand tonight before leaving, rather than relying on memory formed weeks earlier during a briefing, which Priyam felt was worth the extra ten minutes it added to an already long evening given how much it could matter if the worst actually happened on the day.

## Wrap-Up

Overall the room felt well prepared by the end, a noticeable improvement over previous years when marshal briefings happened informally in the group chat rather than in person with a practical exercise attached. Tomasz will send a summary document with position assignments, radio channel details, and the emergency contact card by email this weekend, so everyone has a written reference beyond tonight's verbal briefing to check the night before. Marshals are asked to arrive forty-five minutes before rollout on the day to collect their radio, laminated card, and hi-vis vest from the registration table before heading to position. Priyam closed by thanking everyone for giving up a weeknight, noting the preparation on display tonight was a big part of why club events have run so smoothly compared to the near-misses discussed at the earlier safety review. A few marshals stayed on afterward, comparing notes and swapping numbers with neighbouring positions in case the buddy system needs using on the day. Tomasz left feeling genuinely confident about Saturday, admitted openly, getting a small laugh given how visibly stressed he'd looked before the exercise settled everyone's nerves, his own included.
