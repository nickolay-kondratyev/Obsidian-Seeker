How to tile a zellige backsplash without losing your mind. Written mostly for future-me, since I know I'll forget half of this by the time we do the bathroom. This covers layout, cutting, setting, and grouting for handmade tile specifically, which behaves very differently from the machine-made stuff we used in past apartments.

## The Full Process

Start by dry-fitting an entire row along the countertop before any thinset touches the wall, because handmade zellige tiles vary in size by as much as two or three millimeters from piece to piece, and if you don't sort them first you'll end up with visibly uneven grout lines that no amount of careful grouting can fix afterward. I spread mine out across the dining table by rough size, three piles, and pulled from each pile in rotation so the variation stays distributed instead of clustering in one bad patch of wall. Mark your centerline first, always, even on a wall that looks perfectly square, because our 1962 walls are not perfectly square and starting from a centerline instead of a corner meant the inevitable unevenness got pushed to the less visible end near the cabinets rather than dead center over the range. Use a laser level if you have one; a four-foot level works but you'll be checking it constantly and it's easy to drift over a run this long without noticing until you step back.

Mix thinset in small batches, no more than you can use in about twenty minutes, because handmade tile needs a slightly wetter mix than standard subway tile to grab properly, and thinset that's started to set up will not spread evenly no matter how much you fight it with the trowel. I used a quarter-inch notched trowel, combed in one direction, and back-buttered every single tile in addition to spreading thinset on the wall, which felt excessive at first but made a real difference in coverage once I pulled a few test tiles to check. Handmade tiles have a slightly wavy, irregular surface on the back, unlike the flat backs of machine tile, so back-buttering fills those low spots and prevents hollow spots that would otherwise show up as a dull sound when you tap the finished wall. Set tiles with a slight twisting motion, not just pressing straight in, to work the thinset into full contact, and check with a level every three or four tiles rather than waiting until you've done a whole row, because zellige has enough natural irregularity that small errors compound fast if you don't catch them early.

Spacing was the part I got wrong on my first attempt years ago in a different house: zellige wants a slightly wider grout line than machine tile, something like an eighth of an inch rather than a sixteenth, both because the tile edges are less uniform and because a hairline joint on irregular tile just looks like a mistake rather than a clean modern line. I used plastic spacers for the first few rows until my eye adjusted, then went by feel after that, which is honestly how most tile setters seem to work once they've done enough of it. Let the thinset cure for at least twenty-four hours before grouting, longer if the room is humid, which ours was thanks to a still-curing coat of plaster patch on the ceiling from an unrelated repair.

For grout, we went with a warm off-white rather than pure white, both to hide the inevitable minor imperfections better and because it reads warmer against the sage cabinets than a stark white would. Sanded grout for anything over an eighth of an inch, unsanded below that; ours was right at the line so I went sanded to be safe, since sanded grout is stronger for slightly wider joints even though it can scratch a soft glaze if you're not careful wiping it down. Apply with a rubber float at a forty-five degree angle, working diagonally across the joints rather than along them, which avoids dragging grout back out of the lines you just filled. Wipe with a barely damp sponge, rinsing constantly, and go back over the whole wall a second time about twenty minutes later once the initial haze has started to form, because zellige's uneven glaze surface holds onto grout haze more stubbornly than flat machine tile does and a single pass never quite gets it all. Let it cure fully, about three days, before sealing, even though the wall looks finished the moment the haze is gone.

## Lessons for Next Time

The biggest mistake I'd flag for future-me: buy ten percent more tile than the calculator says, not five, because handmade zellige has a higher breakage rate during cutting than machine tile, and ordering a second small batch mid-project means waiting on a dye lot that may not match. Also budget more time than feels reasonable; this backsplash took roughly three full weekends including the [[049-refinishing-kitchen-cabinets]] work happening in parallel, and rushing any single step shows immediately in irregular grout lines.
