My go-to setup for clean white-background product shots, refined over dozens of catalog jobs. This isn't the only way to get there, but it's fast, repeatable, and doesn't require anything exotic. Works for most small to mid-size products; larger furniture pieces need a different rig entirely, covered elsewhere. ## The Full Setup Walkthrough

Start with the surface itself, since everything else depends on it. I use a seamless white paper roll curved into a cove rather than a hard corner, taped down at the base so it doesn't shift mid-shoot, which happens more often than you'd expect once a product gets set down and picked back up a dozen times during a session. The curve matters because a hard corner creates a visible line in the background that takes real time to clone out in editing, and avoiding that line at the shooting stage saves far more time than fixing it afterward ever does. For genuinely reflective products, glass, polished metal, glossy plastic, I'll sometimes swap the paper for a plexiglass sweep instead, since paper picks up texture under close inspection that plexiglass doesn't, and that texture becomes obvious the moment a client zooms in on a listing image.

Lighting is a three-light setup in almost every case: two strobes for the product itself, positioned at roughly forty-five degrees on either side, and a third dedicated purely to lighting the background evenly enough that it blows out to pure white without any of that light spilling forward onto the product and flattening its shadows in a way that reads as artificial. Getting the background light right is honestly the single biggest lever for how much cleanup a shot needs afterward. Underlit and the background reads as grey rather than white, meaning a heavier edit to bring it up; overlit and light spills onto the product edges, creating a faint halo that's genuinely difficult to correct cleanly without also softening real detail along the product's silhouette. I meter the background separately from the product every single time rather than trusting it looks right by eye, because eyes adjust to a bright scene in ways a histogram simply doesn't, and what looks fine on the back of the camera has fooled me more than once into thinking a background was properly blown out when it wasn't.

For the product lights themselves, softboxes rather than bare strobes almost always, since the softer light wraps more naturally and requires less compositing work later to blend shadow edges convincingly. I keep one light slightly stronger than the other, usually by about a stop, to maintain a sense of dimension rather than flattening the product into a shadowless, characterless blob, which is a common beginner mistake that makes a product look artificial rather than genuinely photographed. For anything with visible texture that actually matters to the buyer, fabric weave, leather grain, brushed metal, I'll sometimes add a small fourth light specifically to rake across that texture at a low angle, since the main product lights alone tend to flatten texture out in a way that undersells exactly the tactile quality a client usually wants emphasized in the final image.

Camera position stays fixed on a tripod for the entire session once it's dialed in, both because consistency across a full batch of products genuinely matters for a client's catalog grid, where inconsistent framing between images looks noticeably unprofessional side by side, and because it means I only have to nail focus and framing once instead of repeatedly for every single item that comes through the set. I shoot tethered directly to a laptop for anything beyond a handful of products, checking focus and histogram on a real screen rather than the camera's small back display, which has genuinely saved more than one shoot from a soft-focus surprise discovered only after the client had already left and the set had been broken down for the day.

Once everything's dialed in on the first product, I try to change as little as possible between subsequent items, only adjusting exposure if a product's own reflectivity or color genuinely demands it, since consistency here is what makes a client's finished catalog look like a coherent, professionally shot set rather than a loose pile of individually decent but visually mismatched images stitched together after the fact by whoever ends up laying out the page.

## Quick Reference

Rough settings I start from:

| Surface | Background | Product lights |
|---|---|---|
| Matte plastic | 1.5 stops over | 45° softboxes |
| Glass / glossy | Plexiglass sweep | 1 stop under |
| Fabric | Standard | Add rake light |
| Jewelry | Tighter cove | Smaller boxes |

Taped near the laptop, a quick glance saves test shots on the first frame. Not a rulebook, more a starting point I adjust once I see how the product behaves under the lights.
