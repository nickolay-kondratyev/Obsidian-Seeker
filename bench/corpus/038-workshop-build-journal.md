Foundation for the workshop went in today, and I spent most of the morning standing at the back fence watching the concrete truck maneuver down our narrow driveway, half convinced it wouldn't fit. It did, barely, with maybe six inches of clearance, and the driver clearly does this every day because he backed the whole thing in without a single correction. The footings had been dug and formed last week, and today's pour filled the perimeter and the two interior pads that will carry the workbench and table saw, both needing more support than a standard slab given the weight and vibration involved. Kofi's crew smoothed the surface with a bull float while it was still workable, then came back an hour later for a finer trowel finish. I kept expecting it to look more dramatic than it did; mostly it was quiet, methodical work, four guys moving across a rectangle of gray that will be the floor I stand on for years of projects, assuming we stay in this house that long, which feels likely given how much we're sinking into it.

## What the Workshop Is For

I've been circling around this question for months without quite answering it directly anywhere, so today, watching the slab cure, felt like a good moment to write it down properly. The short version is that I want a dedicated space for woodworking that isn't the garage, which has never really worked as a shop since it's also where the bikes, the lawn equipment, and roughly a decade of accumulated boxes all live, competing for the same twenty square feet of usable floor. The workshop needs to fit a table saw, a decent-sized workbench, dust collection that won't drive the neighbors up the wall, and enough wall space for the hand tool collection I've been slowly building since well before this house was even on our radar as buyers. Beyond the practical list, though, there's something I want that's harder to put into a budget line: a space that's mine in a way no other room in the house quite is, where a half-finished project can sit on the bench for a week without anyone needing the table for dinner. Priyam asked me recently why I didn't just rent a shared shop space instead of building one, which is a fair question, and the honest answer is that I want to be able to walk out the back door at ten at night if I can't sleep and cut a few dovetails, which isn't really compatible with a shared facility on someone else's schedule. It's also, if I'm being honest, partly about permanence: a workshop feels like a statement that we're staying, in a way that's harder to say out loud than it is to pour into concrete.

## Numbers Today

Fourteen cubic yards of concrete, four hours of pour and finish work, and one genuinely impressive piece of driveway navigation by the truck driver, whose name I never got but should probably try to next time, since this is likely the biggest single pour we'll see on this whole project. Total cost for the slab came in almost exactly on the estimate Kofi gave back in the winter, which felt like a small victory worth noting given how little else has landed on budget so far.

## Waiting on Cure

The slab needs a full week before anyone walks on it with real weight, longer before framing can start stacking material on top of it, so this is mostly a waiting period now, which feels strange after months where everything felt urgent and time-pressured in a way that concrete simply refuses to be rushed on. I've been using the downtime to finalize the electrical plan with the same electrician the kitchen job used, since he already has a sense of our house's quirks and it saves re-explaining the panel situation from scratch, and the workshop's own subpanel sizing question came up again after last week's permit meeting, so that's not fully settled either. I also spent an evening just reading through old woodworking forum threads about shop layout, trying to sketch where the table saw and workbench actually go relative to the garage door opening, since getting that wrong means fighting the space forever afterward in a way that's expensive to undo once walls and outlets are in. Lotte stopped by yesterday and asked, half joking, whether I'd let her use it for pottery glazing once it's done, and I said probably, as long as the dust and the glaze chemicals stay strictly on opposite sides of the room from each other, since that seems like a reasonable line to hold even between friends. There's a version of this project where the workshop becomes a genuinely shared neighborhood resource rather than just mine, and I find I don't mind that idea at all, provided the table saw stays under my exclusive supervision for obvious reasons involving fingers. Kofi, overhearing all this while checking the forms before the pour, just laughed and said every workshop owner says the same thing in year one and none of them mean it by year three, which is probably fair and worth remembering later, and maybe worth writing down here specifically so I can check back on it once the place is finished and full of sawdust.
