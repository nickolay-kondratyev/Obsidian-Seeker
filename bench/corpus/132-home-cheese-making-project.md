After a year of sourdough and vegetable ferments, cheese feels like the natural next step, and it's been sitting on my someday list since Lotte brought a wheel of her own farmhouse cheddar to last spring's canning swap. Unlike bread or kimchi, cheese needs dedicated equipment before a first attempt is possible, plus a more reliable milk source than the supermarket shelf offers. This document is the setup plan before cheesemaking starts, covering equipment, milk sourcing, recipes, and budget.

## Equipment needed

The essentials are a dairy thermometer accurate to a tenth of a degree, cheesecloth, a large stainless pot that isn't reactive with acid, a cheese press for harder styles, and food-grade calcium chloride for store milk that's been pasteurized enough to affect curd set. Rennet, either animal or vegetable, is the other non-negotiable, along with citric acid for quicker fresh cheeses like a simple queso fresco or paneer style. I already own the pot and a reasonably accurate thermometer from candy-making years ago, which cuts the shopping list down more than I expected going in. A cheese mold, likely a basic cylindrical one to start, rounds out the list, along with a proper cheese cave setup eventually, though that's a later phase, not part of this initial build.

## Milk sourcing

Store milk works for fresh cheeses but ultra-pasteurization, common on most supermarket shelves, can prevent a proper curd from forming at all in aged styles, so sourcing matters more here than in most of my other kitchen projects. There's a small dairy about forty minutes out that sells raw milk directly, which Lotte has used for her cheddar with good results, though the drive is a real cost in both time and gas that I need to weigh against convenience. A closer option is a regular grocery store brand that's pasteurized but not ultra-pasteurized, which should work fine for the early fresh-cheese phase while I decide whether the farm drive is worth committing to for the harder styles later. Starting with the closer, easier milk source keeps the first few attempts lower-stakes while I'm still learning basic technique, and I can graduate to the farm drive once I'm confident enough in the process that a wasted batch of good raw milk would actually sting rather than just being an expected part of learning.

## First recipes

Starting with paneer, since it needs no rennet or aging and gives fast feedback on basic curdling technique within an hour. Ricotta next, similarly forgiving. Once those feel consistent, moving to a simple fresh chevre-style cheese with a light culture and short set time, then eventually a pressed cheddar-style once the press and cave setup exist.

## Budget

Rough estimate: thermometer already owned, cheesecloth and citric acid under $15 combined, rennet around $10, calcium chloride $8, a basic cheese mold $20, and a simple press built from scrap wood and weights rather than bought outright, saving real money there. Total for phase one sits comfortably under $60, with the cave setup and any raw milk sourcing costs deferred to a later phase once the fundamentals feel solid enough to justify the bigger investment. That deferred phase, once it happens, will likely add another $80-120 for a proper temperature-controlled cave, most of it going toward a used mini fridge and controller similar to the proofing box build, plus whatever the first few months of raw milk runs actually cost once I commit to the farm drive instead of the closer grocery store option I'm starting with.
