Your FI number is the portfolio size that lets you stop relying on a paycheck, calculated from actual annual spending rather than income, which trips up people who start from the wrong side of the equation. It's a single number, but getting an honest one takes more care than the popular shorthand suggests.

## The full calculation

Start with annual spending, not income, since spending is what a withdrawal has to cover, not what you happened to earn while working. Pull the last twelve months of actual spending from your tracking system, whether that's the zero-based budget described in [[214-build-a-zero-based-budget]] or a simpler bank-statement average, and use the real number rather than a rounded-down guess that flatters your current lifestyle. Most people underestimate this on the first pass because they forget annual and semi-annual expenses that don't show up in a typical month: car registration, an annual subscription paid yearly instead of monthly, gifts during the holidays, an insurance premium that bills twice a year instead of monthly like everything else. Go back through a full calendar year of statements if you can, not just a recent typical month multiplied by twelve, since a typical month systematically misses the lumpy annual stuff that adds up more than people expect once it's all accounted for properly.

Once you have an honest annual spending number, divide it by your target withdrawal rate to get the FI number itself. The commonly cited 4% rule implies dividing by 0.04, which is the same as multiplying spending by 25, giving you the famous "25x annual expenses" shorthand that shows up everywhere in FIRE writing. But 4% was derived from historical US market data over a roughly 30-year retirement horizon, and if you're retiring meaningfully earlier than a traditional retirement age, your money needs to last longer, which argues for a more conservative withdrawal rate and therefore a bigger multiple. Many early-retirement planners use 3.5% (about 28.5x expenses) or even 3.25% (about 30.8x) specifically to account for the longer horizon and the added uncertainty of decades more sequence-of-returns risk than the original studies modeled. We've been using 3.8% ourselves, roughly 26.3x annual expenses, as a middle-ground choice that isn't maximally conservative but also isn't relying on the thinnest possible historical margin, adjusted slightly for our own risk tolerance after several long conversations about it, some of which are logged in [[213-early-retirement-doubts]].

There are refinements worth knowing about even if you don't need all of them on a first pass. A "coast FI" number is smaller: it's the amount that would grow to your full FI number by traditional retirement age through market returns alone, without any further contributions, which tells you when you could stop saving for retirement specifically and only need to cover current living expenses from ongoing income instead. Some people also calculate a "barebones FI" number using a stripped-down austerity budget as a psychological floor, distinct from their actual target, mostly as a worst-case reference point rather than an actual plan they intend to live by day to day once they get there. And healthcare deserves separate, careful treatment rather than being folded into a generic expense category, since pre-Medicare-age healthcare costs in the US specifically can vary enormously depending on state, plan, and household health situation, easily becoming one of the single largest line items in an early retiree's budget and deserving its own dedicated research pass rather than a rough guess borrowed from someone else's number.

Recalculate this number periodically, at minimum every year, since both your spending and your assumptions about withdrawal rate can shift meaningfully over time as life circumstances change. Treat it as a living target you check in on regularly rather than a number you compute once in a burst of enthusiasm and then never revisit again, since a stale number calculated three years ago against an outdated spending pattern is worse than useless, it's actively misleading and can send you toward a decision point with false confidence in a figure that no longer reflects your actual life or actual costs.

## Why this matters more than it seems

Getting the number wrong in either direction has real consequences: too low and you retire into a shortfall you discover years too late to easily fix; too high and you work years longer than necessary, trading time you can't get back for a safety margin you didn't actually need. Treat the calculation as worth revisiting carefully, not a formula you plug in once and trust blindly forever without questioning the underlying assumptions ever again, however tempting that shortcut is.
