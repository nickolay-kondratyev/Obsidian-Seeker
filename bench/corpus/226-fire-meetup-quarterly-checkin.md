# FIRE meetup — quarterly check-in

**Date:** 2026-01-24
**Location:** Kofi's kitchen table, potluck style
**Attendees:** Dana, Priyam, Kofi, Lotte, Tomasz (me facilitating notes this round)

Priyam started this meetup two years ago to hold each other accountable outside the anonymous forums, and it's become the most useful hour of my month. We rotate hosting and note-taking, and everyone brings one real number to share. Tonight's theme was "the boring middle" — the stretch of a FIRE plan where the excitement of the first spreadsheet has worn off but the finish line is still years away. Lotte proposed it after admitting she'd skipped logging her spending for three weeks and felt guilty bringing it up, which set an honest tone. Attendance was full, and Tomasz brought his usual spreadsheet printouts even though we've told him we're happy with a phone screen.

## Individual updates

Dana and I went first since we hosted last quarter. Net worth is up 6.8% over the quarter, mostly market growth rather than new contributions, since December was heavy on holiday travel to see her parents. We're still tracking toward the plan from [[216-annual-financial-planning-meeting]], though the [[217-emergency-fund-rebuild-plan]] is behind schedule by about six weeks because of a car repair. I mentioned the [[213-early-retirement-doubts]] entry and got knowing nods — the 5am spiral is common enough that Lotte has a name for it, "the accountant hour."

Priyam gave the most detailed update. She's now four years from her target date and just crossed a milestone where her dividend and interest income alone covers her rent, which she called "the first time the math felt real instead of theoretical." She's shifted allocation slightly more conservative, moving five percent from equities into a bond ladder, debated since reading about sequence-of-returns risk.

Kofi is earlier in his journey, maybe eight years out, and spent his update on a debt question — accelerate a loan payoff or keep funneling cash into his brokerage account given the loan's rate is lower than his expected return. Nobody had a clean answer.

Lotte's update was the most vulnerable one. She's had a rough quarter emotionally, not financially — a breakup meant renegotiating shared expenses, and her timeline slipped by roughly eighteen months. She was bracing for judgment and got none. Tomasz noted an eighteen-month slip on a fifteen-year plan is well within normal variance, which visibly helped.

Tomasz's own update was brief and upbeat: on track, no surprises, currently optimizing his contributions before the filing deadline, and wanting the group's opinion on backdoor conversion timing, tabled below.

## Discussion: staying motivated in the boring middle

This was the meat of the night. Priyam opened by describing her own experience of the middle years as the hardest part precisely because there's no dramatic before-and-after to point to, just slow compounding that doesn't feel like progress week to week. She said she started keeping a "boring wins" log — small non-financial evidence that the plan is working, like turning down a stressful project at work without panic because she has runway now.

Dana pushed back gently, saying that for us the antidote hasn't been logging wins but shrinking the time horizon we think about day to day; focusing on this month's budget rather than the fifteen-year arc keeps the scale human-sized. I agreed but admitted I still find myself doing napkin math on retirement dates most weeks, which the group teased me about affectionately.

Kofi asked whether anyone else struggles with lifestyle creep sneaking in during the boring middle, since the initial sacrifice-and-adrenaline phase wears off and small indulgences start feeling earned. Lotte said this was exactly what happened to her spending in the last month, and that naming it out loud tonight already felt like it broke some of the spell. Tomasz, characteristically, suggested a structural fix rather than a willpower one: automating an even higher percentage of each paycheck straight into investments before it hits the checking account, so there's simply less discretionary money around to creep.

We spent a while debating whether Tomasz's approach works for everyone or mostly for people who are already disciplined and just want fewer decisions, versus people like Lotte who might do better with more visible, flexible tracking that adapts to life changes rather than a rigid automated system that doesn't know a breakup happened. No firm conclusion, but a good airing of two legitimate philosophies. Priyam closed the discussion by suggesting we each try one small experiment before next quarter — hers will be sharing her "boring wins" log with the group monthly instead of just herself, to see if visibility helps sustain it.

## Backdoor conversion question (Tomasz)

Tomasz wanted a gut check before he acts alone. He's maxed his regular tax-advantaged contributions and is considering a backdoor conversion this year, but is nervous about the pro-rata rule tripping him up since he has an old rollover balance sitting in a traditional account from a previous employer. Priyam, who did this two years ago, walked through her own experience and the mistake she almost made assuming the pro-rata calculation only looked at new contributions rather than her full aggregate balance across every account she holds, including ones she'd half forgotten about from a job she left in her twenties.

Dana asked the obvious follow-up: does the pro-rata rule reset each year, or does an old balance haunt every future conversion until it's dealt with. Priyam wasn't fully sure and admitted she'd had to look it up twice before it stuck, which made Tomasz visibly relax a little — he'd assumed he was missing something everyone else already understood.

The group's rough consensus: Tomasz should either roll the old balance into his current employer's plan first, if it accepts incoming rollovers, or accept the pro-rata tax hit consciously rather than by surprise at filing time. Nobody in the room is a tax professional, and we said so clearly and more than once, but the shared experience was useful enough that Tomasz left with a plan and a shortlist of better questions to bring to someone qualified. He promised to report back next quarter either way, and Kofi half-joked that he wanted a follow-up spreadsheet, which got the biggest laugh of the night.
