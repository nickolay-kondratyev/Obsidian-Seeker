This is my working method for quoting a new product shoot, refined over two years of getting it wrong in both directions: underpricing out of nerves and overpricing out of guesswork. It's not a formula so much as an order of questions to ask before typing a number into an email, and it's saved me from a few bad bookings already.

## Working Through a Quote

Start with the shot count, not the day rate, because that's the number clients actually understand and the one they'll compare against other quotes. Ask specifically: how many distinct products, how many angles per product, and whether any need special treatment like ghost mannequin, 360 spins, or lifestyle context shots mixed into an otherwise flat-lay set. A client who says "about twenty products" often means twenty SKUs at three angles each, which is sixty final images, not twenty. Get this clarified in writing before quoting anything, because the gap between what a client says and what they mean is where most scope creep starts.

Once I have a real shot count, I estimate setup time separately from shooting time, because these scale differently. A single consistent setup, same background, same lighting, same camera position, can produce forty or fifty images in a day once it's dialed in. But if products vary wildly in size or reflectivity, glassware next to matte ceramics next to small jewelry, the setup has to be reworked multiple times during the day, and that eats hours that don't show up in a naive per-image estimate. I keep a rough mental table: flat consistent products, ninety seconds per final image once lit; mixed or reflective products, three to five minutes each; anything requiring a prop change or model, ten-plus minutes. I multiply through and round up, always up, because I have never once regretted padding an estimate and have regretted the opposite more than once.

Editing time gets its own line, separately, because it's invisible to clients and the easiest place to quietly lose money. Basic color correction and background cleanup on a simple flat-lay runs maybe three to five minutes per image once you're fast at it. Anything needing clipping paths, reflection retouching on glass or metal, or heavy dust and scratch removal on used or vintage items can run fifteen to twenty minutes per image, and clients almost never volunteer that their products need this level of cleanup, you find out during the shoot. I now ask directly: "are these new retail samples or will there be dust, fingerprints, or packaging marks to clean up," and the answer changes the quote meaningfully often enough that it's worth the slightly awkward question every time.

From there I build the number bottom-up: day rate covering studio time and my labor, plus a per-image editing rate, plus a small buffer, usually ten percent, for the inevitable one or two reshoots a shoot this size tends to need. I present it to the client as a single project total rather than an hourly breakdown, because hourly breakdowns invite negotiation on the wrong line items, usually the day rate, when the real cost driver is almost always the editing volume. If a client pushes back on the total, I offer to reduce shot count or editing complexity rather than reducing the rate, which keeps the value proposition intact and has, more than once, led the client to realize they didn't actually need all sixty images in the first place.

One more thing I've learned the hard way: I never quote off a phone call alone anymore. I ask for a written brief, even a rough one, plus reference images of similar work they've liked, before the number goes out. Verbal scope has a way of drifting between the call and the shoot day, and having something in writing to point back to has saved more than one relationship when a client's memory of "what we discussed" turned out to be considerably larger than what I'd actually priced. It also gives me something concrete to attach to the invoice later if the final job matches the brief, which makes disputes about scope almost nonexistent compared to my first year, when I quoted almost everything off a five-minute phone call and regretted it more often than not.

## A Quick Checklist

Before sending any quote, I run through: shot count confirmed in writing, product condition confirmed, reference images received, editing complexity estimated per image type, and a ten percent buffer added for reshoots. If any of those five are still fuzzy, I hold the quote and ask one more question rather than guessing, since a day spent clarifying scope up front is cheaper than a shoot that runs long.
