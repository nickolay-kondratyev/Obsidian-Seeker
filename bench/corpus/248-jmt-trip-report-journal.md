Home from the John Muir Trail section, nine days, and my knees are informing me in no uncertain terms that the descents were the real workout, not the climbs I spent all that training time worrying about. Trailhead to trailhead we covered about eighty-four miles, starting at Tuolumne Meadows and finishing at the Whitney Portal, which still doesn't feel real when I type it out like that. The first three days were the hardest mentally, not physically, since my body settled into the rhythm faster than my head did, and I kept catching myself doing trip math instead of just being present on the trail. By day four something shifted and I stopped counting miles left and just started noticing things: the exact blue of Thousand Island Lake at seven in the morning, the sound of wind through the whitebark pines above ten thousand feet, a marmot that followed our camp for twenty minutes at Evolution Meadow clearly hoping for food we weren't going to give it.

## The Group Comes Together

Solo for the first two days before meeting up with Priyam and Kofi at Red's Meadow, which turned out to be exactly the right call, since I needed that alone time more than I realized going in. There's a specific kind of clarity that shows up around day two of solo hiking, once the initial noise of ordinary life finally quiets down and you're just walking, eating, and sleeping on a simple loop with nothing else demanding attention. I thought about work exactly twice the entire trip, both times briefly, which tells me something about how much mental space that job usually occupies without me really noticing until it's gone. Meeting the others at Red's Meadow felt like a reset in a good way, new energy, someone else's jokes, a different pace to match, and the trail dynamic shifted from introspective to genuinely fun almost immediately. We fell into an easy rhythm together: Kofi usually leads in the mornings when he's freshest, I take over in the afternoon once the group slows down, and Priyam is consistently the one pushing us to stop for the view we'd otherwise walk past. Camp routines got efficient fast, everyone with an assigned job by day three without anyone actually assigning them, which is one of my favorite things about hiking with people you've done this with before. There's an unspoken shorthand that develops on trips like this, where you can tell from someone's pace or how quiet they've gotten whether they need a break or just need to push through to the next water source.

## Wildlife

Wildlife highlights: a black bear at a distance near Sallie Keyes Lakes, unbothered by us and gone within a minute, plus more marmots than I could count and one very bold deer that walked straight through our camp at Guitar Lake. Weather held for all nine days, which given the stories we'd heard about afternoon thunderstorms felt like genuine luck rather than good planning on our part. A few distant coyote calls one night near Rae Lakes rounded out the list.

## Whitney and the Last Day

Mount Whitney on the last day deserves its own entry, honestly, but the short version: alpine start at two in the morning, headlamps for the first three hours, and a summit at sunrise that none of us will forget for a long time. The final push above thirteen thousand feet was slow and deliberate, breathing hard even on flat sections, a reminder of how much altitude changes the math on effort no matter how fit you think you are going in. Standing at the summit as the sun came up over the eastern Sierra, watching the shadow of the peak stretch out across the desert below, was one of those moments that justifies every blister and every hard mile that got us there. The descent to Whitney Portal afterward was brutal in its own way, eleven miles of knee-jarring downhill on tired legs, and I don't think any of us said much for the last two hours except the occasional groan at another switchback. We celebrated at the burger place near the portal with the kind of hunger that only nine days on trail can produce, and Kofi ordered two entrees without a hint of embarrassment, which felt entirely earned. Already thinking about what's next; Priyam mentioned the Sierra High Route as only half joking, and I have a feeling that's going to become a real plan by winter. See [[252-sierra-high-route-project]] for where that idea is starting to take shape, and [[247-appalachian-trail-section-project]] for the shorter trip we're doing before anything that ambitious. For now I'm just sitting with the trip, letting it settle before I start planning the next one, which never lasts more than a few days before the maps come back out anyway. There's a particular kind of tiredness after a trip like this that's different from ordinary exhaustion, a mix of physical depletion and something closer to grief for the simplicity of trail life, both of which fade slowly over the next week or two as ordinary routines reassert themselves.

## Recovery

Recovery notes for future me: two rest days minimum before any real exercise, and expect the knees to complain for about a week after a trip with this much sustained downhill. Laundry first, gear check second, journal entry third, in that order, always, no matter how tired I am when I walk in the door.
