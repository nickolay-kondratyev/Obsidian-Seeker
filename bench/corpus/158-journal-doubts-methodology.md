## The comparison problem

The thing that's really eating at me is comparing my design to what the advisor clearly expects. In our last meeting she talked about "triangulation" like it was obvious — survey data cross-checked against interview themes cross-checked against agency records. I nodded along and wrote it down, but I don't actually know how to operationalize triangulation in a way that isn't just me picking whichever finding I already believed going in. Is that confirmation bias dressed up in methods language? I reread two methodology textbooks this week looking for a clean answer and both of them describe triangulation in ways that sound rigorous in the abstract and completely hand-wavy once you try to apply them to an actual messy dataset with 40 survey responses and 12 interviews. Twelve interviews. Is that even enough to say anything about a whole transit agency's culture? I don't know, and not knowing is starting to feel less like a normal research problem and more like evidence I picked the wrong approach from the start. I keep imagining the defense room, someone on the committee asking why twelve and not twenty, and me not having an answer beyond "that's when I ran out of scheduling slots and funding for transcription." That's not a methodological justification, that's a logistics excuse dressed up afterward, and I suspect any careful reader would spot the seam immediately. Maybe the honest move is to say the sample size explicitly in the limitations section instead of hoping no one asks, but even writing that sentence makes my stomach drop a little.

## Trying to talk myself down

Okay. Writing this out because journaling is supposedly what helps and I have nothing to lose at this hour. First: the advisor approved this design in September, in writing, over email, and she has read four drafts of the proposal since then without flagging the mixed-methods logic as a problem. I think what I actually want isn't a methodologically perfect design, I want a guarantee that no one will ever question it, and that guarantee doesn't exist for any method, quantitative or qualitative or mixed. Third: twelve interviews is thin for a standalone qualitative study but it's reasonable as one leg of a triangulated design, especially paired with agency documents I haven't even started coding yet. I think the panic tonight is partly about the interviews and partly just about being tired and behind on the transcription backlog, which has a way of making everything else feel more precarious than it actually is.
