# Notes on wild yeast, before and after we understood it

For most of the history of bread, nobody knew what was actually happening inside a bowl of flour and water left out overnight. Bakers understood that some batches of dough behaved differently than others, that a bit of yesterday's dough saved and mixed into today's would reliably make bread rise, and that certain kitchens seemed to produce more reliable results than others, but the mechanism was invisible and largely irrelevant to the practice. Bread got made this way for thousands of years, across cultures with no contact with each other, arriving independently at roughly the same solution: save a piece of the last batch, feed it, use it again. It's one of those quietly remarkable facts about food history that the technique converged so consistently without anyone understanding the biology behind it, purely through observation and the discipline of not throwing away something that had clearly worked.

It took until the mid-1800s for Louis Pasteur's work on fermentation to establish that living microorganisms, not some vague chemical process, were responsible for both the rise in bread and the sourness in aged dough. Before that, competing theories ranged from spontaneous generation to chemical explanations that, in hindsight, got the observable effects roughly right while missing the actual cause. Pasteur's identification of yeast as a living organism metabolizing sugars into carbon dioxide and alcohol reframed baking into something with a describable mechanism, even if most home bakers kept doing what they'd always done, uninterested in the science now available.

The twentieth century added the second half of the picture: lactic acid bacteria, living alongside wild yeast in the same starter culture, responsible for the sour flavor yeast alone doesn't produce. This is the detail that made commercial baker's yeast, isolated and sold as a single reliable strain starting in the late 1800s, a genuinely different product from a traditional starter rather than a purified version of the same thing. Commercial yeast rises bread fast and predictably but skips bacterial fermentation entirely, which is why a same-day commercial loaf and a naturally leavened sourdough loaf, despite both technically being "yeasted," taste and behave so differently even with identical flour and water.

## What's actually happening in the jar

A mature sourdough starter is a working ecosystem, not a single organism, typically dominated by a handful of wild yeast species alongside several strains of lactic acid bacteria, all competing for the same sugars released as enzymes break down the flour. The specific balance between species varies by starter, shaped by flour type, ambient temperature, feeding schedule, and even the particular strains already present in a given kitchen's air and surfaces, which is part of why two starters fed identically in different homes can behave and taste noticeably differently within weeks. The yeast produce carbon dioxide, giving the rise; the bacteria produce lactic and acetic acid, giving the tang, and the ratio between those two acids is largely what separates a mild, yogurt-like starter from a sharp, vinegary one.

## Why rye starts faster

Rye flour carries more naturally occurring wild yeast and bacteria on the bran than white flour, plus more readily available sugars for early microbial activity, which is the biological reason rye starters typically show visible activity faster than all-purpose ones in the first several days, something borne out repeatedly in casual side-by-side comparisons among home bakers regardless of kitchen or region. Whole wheat sits somewhere between the two, carrying more bran-associated microbial life than refined white flour but less than rye, tracking with the middling activity speed most bakers report starting a whole wheat culture. Refined white flour, stripped of most bran and germ during milling, offers the fewest resident microorganisms and least available food, which is exactly why a white flour starter often takes longer to show its first real bubbles, discouraging first-timers who assume nothing is happening at all. This is also part of why many guides recommend beginning with rye or whole wheat even when the plan is eventually maintaining on white flour going forward.

## Capturing versus cultivating

Every starter begins as a capture — flour and water left out, exposed to whatever wild yeast and bacteria happen to be present in the air, on the flour itself, and on kitchen surfaces nearby. But what survives past the first week isn't really "wild" in any meaningful ongoing sense; it's been selected and shaped by the specific feeding regimen imposed on it, favoring whichever organisms handle that particular schedule, hydration, and temperature best. Two starters captured from the same kitchen on the same day, fed differently afterward, will diverge into genuinely different microbial communities within a month, which is part of why "authentic" claims about starter origin matter less than people sometimes assume; the daily feeding routine reshapes the culture far more than its original capture conditions ever did. This has real implications for the folklore around gifted starters — a scoop of someone else's decade-old culture, once it's living in your kitchen and being fed on your schedule, will drift toward a population shaped by your conditions rather than staying a fixed inheritance from wherever it originally came from. Within a few months, a "hundred-year-old" starter maintained in a new kitchen is arguably more a product of that kitchen's specific yeast and bacteria than of whatever culture it supposedly descended from, even though the story travels with the jar regardless, which says something about how much folklore and biology can quietly diverge from each other while the label stays the same. None of that makes the story less worth telling, only less literally true than it's usually presented as being.

## A humbling thought

Every loaf, in some sense, is a report on conditions nobody can fully see or control — a readout of a kitchen's particular invisible population, expressed in crumb and crust rather than numbers on a screen.
