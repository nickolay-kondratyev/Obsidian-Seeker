Decided this week is dedicated entirely to async, no new project work, just reading and small isolated exercises until the fog clears at least a little. I've been avoiding it for two months now, ever since chapter seventeen of the book flagged it as an advanced topic and I quietly decided "advanced" meant "later," which in practice has meant "never" every single time I've reached that chapter boundary and instinctively closed the book instead of pushing through it like I have with every harder-than-expected topic so far. But the file watcher project and the key-value store both keep bumping into places where async would genuinely simplify things, particularly around concurrent network requests I keep working around with threads instead, so later has officially arrived and I can't keep deferring it any longer without the debt actively slowing down real project work. Sunday night I blocked out the whole week on my calendar, which felt a little dramatic for a self-directed hobby project, but past experience says vague intentions to "get to it eventually" reliably lose to whatever's more urgent that day.

## Monday and Tuesday: the mental model shift

Started Monday with the async chapter of the book plus supplementary blog posts Dana sent over, since she said the book's async coverage is notoriously thin compared to how central the topic actually is to most production Rust work happening in industry right now. First impression: the syntax itself, `async fn` and `.await`, looks deceptively similar to synchronous code, almost suspiciously so, but the mental model underneath is completely different in ways the surface similarity actively works against rather than helps with, since it lulls you into assuming familiar reasoning applies when it mostly doesn't transfer cleanly at all. An `async fn` doesn't run when called; it returns a `Future` that does nothing at all until something actually polls it, which is a genuinely strange inversion coming from synchronous code where calling a function always means it starts running immediately without any separate trigger needed.

Spent Tuesday specifically on the relationship between futures and executors, since that inversion from Monday kept tripping me up in every subsequent example I tried working through on my own. A `Future` in Rust is just a state machine, generated by the compiler from your `async fn` body, that gets polled repeatedly until it returns `Poll::Ready` instead of `Poll::Pending`. The executor, `tokio` in basically every example I found, drives that polling loop, deciding when to poll each future again after being woken. This is different enough from JavaScript's promises, which start executing immediately on creation, that I had to consciously unlearn a full decade of assuming "async equals JavaScript promises" before Rust's model actually made sense to me on its own terms rather than as a variation on something I already thought I understood.

## Wednesday: writing something real

Built a tiny program that fetches three URLs concurrently instead of sequentially, using `reqwest` and `tokio::join!`, mostly to see the model pay off in a genuinely observable, timed way rather than staying abstract in my head. Sequential fetches took roughly three seconds total; concurrent fetches took about one second, matching the slowest request, exactly the speedup the chapter had promised without a stopwatch to back it up. Ran it five times just to be sure the numbers held and weren't a fluke of network conditions that evening.

## Thursday and Friday: where I'm still stuck

Seeing the wall clock number drop on Wednesday made the whole concept click in a way pure reading hadn't managed after two days of study, the same pattern I keep noticing this year: reading gets me partway there, but a concrete number on a stopwatch is what cements a concept into something I trust rather than something I merely believe because a book told me so. Thursday's target was pinning, and it did not go nearly as well. I read the explanation three separate times and still can't confidently explain why some futures need `Pin<Box<...>>` while others don't, or what self-referential structs have to do with any of it in a way I could teach back to someone else. The book's example, a struct holding both a value and a reference into that same value, makes sense on the page, but I lose the thread the moment I try to extend it to my own code, particularly around why moving such a struct in memory would actually be unsafe rather than merely inconvenient for the borrow checker to reason about, and every explanation I found online seemed to assume I already understood the exact thing it was trying to explain in the first place, which made each new attempt feel like starting over from zero rather than building on the last one. Friday I gave up trying to fully understand pinning from first principles and instead just memorized the pattern: wrap it in `Box::pin`, move on, revisit later once more of the surrounding vocabulary has settled in through repeated exposure. Bookmarked for the pairing session with Dana next week, see [[076-pairing-on-lifetimes]] for how well the box-drawing approach worked on lifetimes, and I'm hoping something similarly concrete and visual works for pinning too, since pure prose explanations clearly aren't landing for this particular concept no matter how many times I reread them slowly on my own without anyone to talk it through with.
