# Why Preserving Your Own Food Still Matters

Every autumn I have some version of the same argument with myself while standing over a pot of boiling tomatoes, wondering whether the hours spent canning are actually worth it when the grocery store sells a perfectly serviceable jar of passata for a few dollars year-round, no lids to sterilize and no risk of a seal failing quietly in the pantry over winter. It's a fair question, and the honest economic answer, most years, is that home canning barely breaks even once you account for the cost of the produce, the jars, the lids, and the hours of standing over a hot stove on what's usually one of the last genuinely warm weekends of the year. And yet I keep doing it, every single September, and I've come to think the economic framing was always the wrong lens for understanding why, since it measures the one dimension that was never actually the point of the exercise in the first place. What the spreadsheet can't capture is the specific, located pleasure of eating a February pasta sauce made from tomatoes I remember picking, sun-warm, off a particular plant in the garden, a kind of continuity between seasons that a supermarket jar, however good, simply cannot offer no matter how carefully sourced its ingredients happen to be. Kofi, who's been canning since before the ferment club existed in any organized form, describes it as eating the calendar rather than eating around it, a phrase that's stuck with me since he first said it standing in his own kitchen surrounded by more jars than any two people could reasonably need for a single winter. There's also something the economic framing misses about resilience, in the unglamorous, practical sense: a pantry with forty jars of preserved food is a genuinely different kind of security than an equivalent amount of money in a bank account, even if the numbers on paper look identical or even favor the money.

## The Skill Argument

Preserving food is, at its core, a set of transferable skills that atrophy quickly without regular use, and every year I don't can something is a year that muscle memory around lid seals and headspace measurements quietly degrades, the same way any physical skill fades without practice. Reading about the history in [[140-wild-yeast-history-article]] made this clearer to me than any modern source had: entire generations once carried this knowledge as a baseline life skill rather than a specialty hobby, and losing it entirely, even in a world with reliable supermarkets, feels like a genuine loss rather than simple, harmless obsolescence. There's a version of this argument that applies to fermentation and baking too, but canning feels sharper somehow, maybe because the margin for error is less forgiving and the skill decays faster when neglected, punishing a rusty summer far more visibly than a slightly under-fed starter ever would.

## The Community Argument

Preserving food alone in a kitchen is one thing; preserving food alongside a dozen other people at a canning swap is something else entirely, and it's the social layer, more than the food itself, that's kept me showing up to every seasonal preserving session the ferment club runs, the same sessions that eventually pushed us to organize the pantry properly in [[147-ferment-pantry-inventory-project]]. Skills pass hand to hand in that setting in a way no written recipe fully captures: someone corrects your water bath timing in real time, someone else notices your jars aren't sealing properly before you'd have caught it yourself three weeks later opening a spoiled jar in December, and the whole afternoon becomes a genuinely collaborative project rather than a solitary chore performed in isolation. Priyam, who joined the club mostly for the bread, says the canning swaps are what actually kept her coming back, since nothing else in her week involves quite that particular mix of shared labor, easy conversation, and a tangible pile of jars to show for an afternoon's work by the time everyone packs up and heads home.

## The Counterargument, Taken Seriously

I don't want to romanticize this past the point of honesty. Home canning done carelessly carries real risk, botulism chief among it, and the skill genuinely does need to be learned properly rather than absorbed by vibes from a relative's half-remembered method passed down without the actual science behind it. It's also, undeniably, a privilege to have the time, the kitchen space, and the garden or market access that make canning practical at all, and I try not to pretend this is some universally accessible act of self-sufficiency rather than a genuinely time-intensive hobby that not everyone can fit into an already full week. The environmental argument for home preserving is also weaker than it's sometimes made out to be once you account for home energy use against industrial-scale efficiency, and I don't think preserving your own food is meaningfully greener than buying canned tomatoes from a well-run commercial operation, whatever the more idealistic corners of the movement sometimes imply about it. None of that changes my own answer, but I think it's worth being honest about which parts of the case are really about values and pleasure rather than measurable, defensible impact on either the wallet or the planet. I'd rather make the pleasure argument directly than dress it up in a resilience or sustainability case that doesn't quite hold once someone actually runs the numbers against it, since an argument that only survives until the first honest audit isn't really an argument worth leaning on at all, however comforting it feels while stirring a pot of tomatoes on a warm September afternoon.

## Standing Over the Pot Again

So here I am again, tomatoes boiling, jars cooling on a towel, doing the math on hours versus dollars and choosing, once again, to ignore what the math says in favor of a shelf of jars I'll be genuinely glad to see in February.
