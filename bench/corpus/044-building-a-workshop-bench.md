How to build a proper workbench for the new workshop, the first real furniture piece to go into the space once the slab cured and framing wrapped. This is a traditional laminated-top bench, roughly the style my grandfather would have recognized, built heavy enough to stay put under hand-plane pressure and years of clamping abuse.

## The Full Build

I started with the top, since everything else in a workbench build takes its cues from how thick and flat that surface ends up. I used construction-grade Douglas fir two-by-sixes, ripped down to remove the rounded edges, then laminated face to face with plenty of glue and enough clamps that I had to borrow a second set from Kofi, whose crew apparently owns more pipe clamps than any reasonable person needs for kitchen cabinetry work. Getting the boards flat before glue-up matters more than I expected going in; any twist or cup in an individual board telegraphs straight through the glue-up and shows up as a stubborn low spot that no amount of hand-planing afterward fully corrects without removing more material than you'd like from an otherwise good top. I let the glue cure a full two days rather than the one the bottle technically called for, mostly out of impatience-adjacent caution, since I only get one shot at this top and didn't want to find out the hard way that twenty-four hours wasn't quite enough for a lamination this thick.

Flattening the glued-up slab came next, and this is where I learned that a benchtop this size genuinely tests your patience with a hand plane, even a well-tuned one. I worked diagonally across the grain first with a jack plane set for a heavier cut, watching for the low spots that show up as untouched patches once you've knocked down the high ridges, then switched to a jointer plane along the grain for the final smoothing pass once the surface read flat under a long straightedge in every direction I checked it. This took the better part of an afternoon and left my forearms sorer than I expected from what's supposedly a finesse task rather than a strength one, though I suspect that says more about my technique than about hand-tool woodworking generally. Once flat, I cut the top to final dimensions, roughly six feet by two and a half, sized to fit comfortably against the workshop's south wall with enough clearance behind it to walk around freely for the awkward angles that always come up mid-project.

The base is a traditional four-leg trestle design, mortise-and-tenon joinery throughout rather than screws or brackets, both because it's genuinely stronger under the kind of racking stress a workbench takes over years of planing and clamping, and because I wanted at least one part of this build to actually use the joinery skills I've been slowly accumulating rather than defaulting to pocket screws the way I have on faster, less precious projects. Cutting the mortises took a router with a simple jig for consistency, followed by hand-chiseled corners to square up what the router's round bit couldn't reach, and the tenons were cut on the table saw with a dado stack, then hand-fit with a shoulder plane until each one seated with the kind of firm, even resistance you want rather than either a loose wobble or a joint you have to hammer home and risk splitting. Assembly was a genuinely tense hour with glue already spread and the clock running, everything needing to come together square and true before the glue started to grab, which it does faster than feels fair once you're wrestling four legs and two long stretchers into alignment simultaneously with only two hands and one increasingly nervous mind.

Once the base was solid, I attached the top with steel bench bolts through the stretchers rather than glue, a deliberate choice that lets me remove the top someday for resurfacing without disassembling the whole bench. I added a leg vise on the front left corner, the side I favor for planing, and a wagon vise at the tail end for holding boards flat during work. Final step was a coat of boiled linseed oil rather than a film finish, letting the wood breathe and darken naturally with use.

## Vises and Finish

Both vises are secondhand, found through a local tool exchange group rather than bought new, which saved a meaningful amount against what a comparable pair would have cost from a specialty catalog, and gave the whole bench a slightly mismatched, well-loved character before it's even seen its first real project. The linseed oil finish needs occasional reapplication, maybe twice a year, but that upkeep felt like a fair trade against something more durable but less forgiving of the dings a working bench inevitably collects over its life.
