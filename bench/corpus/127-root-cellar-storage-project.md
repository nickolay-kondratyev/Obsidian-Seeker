Converting the unused basement corner into proper cold storage for squash, potatoes, and the growing stack of canning jars. Everything currently sits on a wire shelf near the furnace, far too warm for winter squash, and has already cost two soft potatoes.

## Why now

The canning habit has outgrown the kitchen cupboard, and squash from the fall garden needs somewhere cooler than the pantry to last past January. Lotte's basement setup, which she's mentioned a few times at [[121-ferment-club-monthly-meeting]], convinced me a cheap shelf-and-insulation approach beats buying anything specialized, especially since her jars have held up fine through two full winters already without a single spoiled batch to report.

## The corner

The northeast basement corner sits furthest from the furnace and already reads a steady 52-55F through winter, based on a week of spot checks with a spare thermometer. It has a concrete floor, which helps with humidity for root vegetables, and enough headroom for two shelf units stacked with room to spare. The only downside is a support column eating into the usable footprint, which the shelf layout will need to work around rather than fight.

## Shelf plan

Two wire shelving units, one for jars and dry goods wanting it cooler and drier, one for squash and roots wanting more humidity. A shallow tray of damp sand on the squash shelf bumps humidity locally, cardboard baffles between units stop it migrating.

## Monitoring

A cheap wireless thermometer-hygrometer with a base station upstairs means I can check conditions without walking down every day, which matters most during the first month while I'm still tuning the setup. Logging morning and evening readings for the first six weeks, then dropping to a weekly spot check once the numbers settle into a predictable pattern I actually trust.

## Timeline and cost

Shelving units are already owned, repurposed from the garage. A thin foam board barrier between the corner and the furnace side of the basement should keep the temperature swing down too, since a furnace cycle noticeably warms the whole room within twenty minutes; cheap and reversible if it doesn't help enough. Foam board, sand tray, and the wireless thermometer together should run under $50, all sourced locally rather than ordered, so the whole project could realistically be finished in a single weekend if the insulation cuts cleanly on the first try.

## Open question

Whether the humidity split between the two shelves actually holds once the sand tray dries out, or whether I'll need a small humidifier instead, which would add both cost and a power outlet requirement I haven't accounted for yet.
