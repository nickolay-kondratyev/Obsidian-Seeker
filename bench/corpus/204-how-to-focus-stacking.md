My process for focus stacking small products, mostly jewelry, where depth of field at true macro distance is razor-thin even stopped down hard, and a single frame simply can't hold enough of a ring or a pendant in sharp focus to satisfy a client looking closely at the final delivered image on their own screen.

## Working Through a Stack

Start by deciding how many frames the stack actually needs, which depends entirely on the subject rather than being a fixed number I apply blindly every time. A flat item like a pressed pendant might need six to eight frames; a ring with real depth, the band, the setting, the stone all at different distances from the lens, can need fifteen or more to get genuinely clean, edge-to-edge sharpness across the whole piece without any soft transition zones visible on close inspection. I always shoot a couple more frames than I think I'll need, since it's much cheaper in time to discard an unnecessary frame in post than to discover a gap in the stack after the shoot has already been broken down and the product has gone back to the client.

Camera stays locked on a tripod for the entire stack, no exceptions, since even a fraction of a millimeter of camera movement between frames will show up as a visible misalignment once the stack gets processed, and manual focus rail adjustments between shots are the only thing that should be moving during the sequence. I use a geared focus rail rather than adjusting focus on the lens itself between frames, since the rail moves the whole camera a fixed, precise, repeatable distance rather than relying on the focus ring's imprecise, somewhat inconsistent throw, which is nowhere near accurate enough for the kind of fine, consistent steps a clean stack actually needs between each successive frame in the sequence. Step size depends on the lens's actual depth of field at the working aperture, typically somewhere in the range of a quarter to half a millimeter of rail movement per frame for true macro work on jewelry, small enough that consecutive frames overlap comfortably in sharp focus, which matters more than most beginners initially expect since gaps between frames show up as soft bands in the final stacked image that are genuinely difficult to fix after the fact without reshooting the whole sequence.

Lighting has to stay completely identical across every single frame in the stack, which sounds obvious stated plainly but is easy to accidentally violate if a reflective surface catches even a slightly different highlight as the camera's physical position shifts incrementally along the rail during the sequence. I check the first and last frame of every stack side by side immediately after shooting, specifically looking for any lighting shift before I break the set down, since catching a lighting problem in the field costs five extra minutes to reshoot properly, while catching the same problem later during editing means either a compromised final image or a genuinely wasted, no-longer-recoverable trip back to a client for a reshoot that eats real goodwill along with real time neither of us wants to spend on a redo.

For processing, I stack in dedicated focus-stacking software rather than trying to do it manually in a general photo editor, since the automated alignment and blending genuinely handles the edge cases, transparent gemstone facets especially, far better than manual layer masking ever could reliably manage on its own. Even so, the automated result almost always needs some manual cleanup afterward, usually around the stone's facets specifically, where the algorithm sometimes picks a slightly soft transition between two adjacent source frames instead of the genuinely sharpest available option, and I've learned to always zoom to 100% and check every facet edge individually rather than trusting the software's blend at a glance from a normal viewing distance, since that's exactly where the eventual client zoom for a jewelry purchase decision tends to land anyway, right on the facets, so it's the one place in the whole image where a stacking artifact is guaranteed to actually get noticed and questioned by someone genuinely paying attention.

## Gear Note

None of this requires exotic equipment: a basic geared focus rail, roughly $60-80, and any decent focus-stacking software, several free or cheap options exist, are enough. The tripod and lens matter more than the rail brand ever will. See [[189-how-to-pack-travel-kit]] for how I pack the rail separately from the rest of the macro kit, since it's small enough to get lost in the bottom of a duffel bag if it isn't kept in its own labeled pouch every single time.
