# On slow food that actually takes time

There's a particular kind of impatience that modern kitchens are built to serve, and fermentation refuses to cooperate with it. You cannot rush a sourdough starter into strength by wanting it badly enough, and you cannot argue a jar of kimchi into sourness a day early no matter how carefully you've read the recipe. This isn't a flaw in the process; it's the entire point, and I think it's worth sitting with why that matters beyond the obvious appeal of a hobby that forces patience onto an otherwise instant-everything life.

Most cooking rewards attention paid in the moment. You watch the pan, you taste as you go, you adjust heat and salt in real time and the dish tells you almost immediately whether you got it right. Fermentation rewards attention paid across days and weeks, in small increments that don't resolve into feedback until much later. You check a starter's rise and won't know if today's feed schedule was right until tomorrow's rise tells you. You taste a ferment on day three and the information you're gathering is only useful for adjusting day four, five, and beyond, never for fixing today. It's a different relationship with cause and effect than almost anything else in a kitchen, and it took me longer than I'd like to admit to stop treating every check-in as a verdict rather than a single data point in a longer story.

I've come to think this is why the ferment club conversations rarely stay purely technical for long. Ask Kofi about salt percentages and within five minutes he's talking about his grandmother's kimchi and the particular way she'd press a hand flat against the crock to check if it was ready, a gesture no recipe card ever taught him and that no recipe card could fully replace. Ask Priyam about starter hydration and she'll eventually mention the two years she spent failing before her first one took, which she describes now with something closer to fondness than frustration. The technical knowledge is real and worth having, but it seems to function as a doorway into something slower and more personal that the technique alone doesn't explain.

## The tyranny of the deadline

Most home cooking optimizes toward a deadline — dinner at seven, guests arriving at six thirty, a lunch that needs packing before the bus leaves. Fermentation has no honest deadline; it has a window, sometimes a wide one, and pretending otherwise is how you end up with kimchi fermented in a rush that never develops real depth, or bread baked from an underfed starter because the calendar said today was the day. Learning to plan around a process that won't be hurried, rather than forcing the process to fit an existing plan, has been the single biggest shift in how I schedule kitchen projects generally, and it has quietly reshaped how I plan the rest of the week around it, treating the starter's schedule as a fixed point everything else has to bend around instead of the reverse.

## What patience actually buys you

The payoff isn't just flavor, though the flavor difference between a rushed and a properly timed ferment is real and noticeable to anyone paying attention. It's also a kind of trust that builds slowly between you and the process — you learn what your kitchen's particular temperature swings do to a starter's timeline, what your specific salt brand's grain size does to brine concentration, details no universal recipe can hand you because they're specific to your exact conditions. That trust doesn't transfer between kitchens either; Lotte's basement setup runs a full ten degrees cooler than my counter, and her timelines are simply different from mine in ways neither of us can shortcut around by reading harder. It also buys a strange kind of calm I didn't expect when I started. Once you accept a ferment will be ready when it's ready, the daily check-in stops feeling like a test you might fail and starts feeling more like checking in on something alive doing its own work at its own pace.

## Where this leaves the impatient cook

None of this is an argument against convenience elsewhere in the kitchen — I'll use a pressure cooker without a second thought and see no contradiction in loving both. Efficiency has its place, and I'm not interested in performing patience as some kind of virtue where it isn't actually needed; a weeknight stir-fry benefits from speed the same way a starter benefits from time, and pretending otherwise would be a different kind of dishonesty about what each process actually wants from you. But fermentation has earned a permanent exemption from my usual impatience, a small deliberate pocket of the week where the answer to "can I speed this up" is simply no, and that answer, unusually, doesn't bother me at all. Part of why it doesn't bother me is that the waiting isn't empty; there's a specific kind of attention in checking a starter's rise or pressing down a floating cabbage leaf that feels less like idle waiting and more like a small ritual, repeated often enough that it's become one of the more grounding parts of an otherwise rushed week. Kofi once described his evening starter check as the only five minutes of his day where he isn't looking at a screen, and while I'd resisted that framing as a little precious when he first said it, I've since caught myself agreeing more than expected. There's also something clarifying about a process that refuses to be negotiated with, in a life otherwise full of things that bend to enough pressure; the starter does not care how badly you need bread by Thursday, and there's real relief in that indifference once you stop fighting it.

## A small confession

I still check the starter more often than necessary most mornings, less for information and more because I like watching it. Some habits outlast their original justification, and this is one I've stopped trying to correct.
