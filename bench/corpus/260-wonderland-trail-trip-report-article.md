Nine days, ninety-three miles, and roughly twenty-two thousand feet of elevation gain around Mount Rainier: a full account of the Wonderland Trail loop, written a couple of weeks after getting home while the memories are still sharp enough to trust. We planned this trip for the better part of a year, starting with the permit lottery in the spring and ending with a gear shakedown two weeks before departure that caught more problems than any of us expected going in. Three of us went: Dana, Priyam, and Kofi, all with prior multi-day experience but nothing at quite this scale or with this much sustained elevation change day after day for over a week straight. The route circles the mountain almost entirely, crossing through old-growth forest, high alpine meadows, and several substantial river valleys carved by glacial meltwater that shifts noticeably in volume and danger depending on the time of day. What struck me most, even before the trip started, was how different this felt from our usual point-to-point trips; a loop has a strange psychological quality, always circling back toward where you started rather than pushing steadily toward a distant endpoint. The first two days took us through dense forest on the south side, humid and green, with only occasional breaks in the canopy offering any real view of the mountain we were technically circling the entire time. By day three, climbing out of the South Puyallup valley, the terrain opened up dramatically, and we got our first real sustained views of Rainier's western face, glaciers catching the afternoon light in a way that made the whole group stop talking for a solid minute. Golden Lakes on day four lived up to its name at sunset, the whole basin lit up in warm color, and it's the camp all three of us agreed afterward was the single best of the trip, worth the extra mileage it took to reach. None of us slept particularly well that first night, adjusting to full pack weight and unfamiliar ground, but by the third night the trail rhythm had settled into something automatic, the kind of routine that only shows up a few days into a longer trip.

## The River Crossing

The middle section of the loop, through Mowich River and up toward Ipsut Pass, was where the trip's only real logistical challenge showed up: a river crossing at Tahoma Creek that runs dangerously high in the afternoon once snowmelt peaks for the day. We'd planned for this in advance, crossing first thing in the morning specifically to avoid the worst of it, and the extra planning paid off completely, a nonevent instead of the genuine hazard it can become later in the day. Kofi, who'd been the most vocal about the crossing risk during planning, admitted afterward it was almost a letdown given how much thought we'd put into timing it correctly, which is honestly the best outcome a known hazard on a trip like this can produce.

## The Halfway Rest Day

Mystic Camp and the approach to Sunrise marked the psychological halfway point of the trip, and it's also where we'd built in our one scheduled rest day, which turned out to be one of the better decisions in the whole plan. Legs needed it by then, four solid days of sustained climbing and descending having taken a real toll, and the extra day let us properly enjoy the alpine meadows around Sunrise instead of just passing through them on tired legs. We day-hiked without packs up toward Frozen Lake, moving faster and lighter than we had in days, and it was a genuine reminder of how much a loaded pack changes the entire experience of walking through a landscape like this one. Priyam spent most of the rest day journaling near camp, and Dana and I spent an embarrassing amount of time just lying in the meadow grass, doing nothing at all, which felt like exactly the right use of a rest day this far into a trip.

## Panhandle Gap and the Final Push

The final third of the loop, from Sunrise through Indian Bar and back to Longmire, brought the trip's other defining challenge: mosquitoes, worse than any of us had experienced on a trip before, thick enough at Indian Bar that we ate dinner inside the tent vestibules rather than face them in the open. Panhandle Gap, the highest point on the route, delivered the trip's most dramatic single view: the whole southern flank of the mountain laid out below us, still patched with snow in early September, glaciers visibly active even from a distance in a way photographs never quite capture. The descent back into Box Canyon and finally to Longmire felt anticlimactic in the way loop trips often do, retracing terrain that was technically new to us but psychologically felt like a return rather than a discovery, the trip's momentum shifting from exploration to simple completion. Finishing at Longmire, the same spot we'd started nine days earlier, was a strange full-circle feeling, standing in the exact parking lot where the whole thing began, gear worn and bodies tired in a way that felt entirely earned rather than merely accumulated. We celebrated with burgers at the lodge restaurant, still in full trail clothes, drawing a few looks from other guests that none of us cared about in the slightest given how good that first real meal tasted after nine days of dehydrated dinners. Looking back at the whole loop now, it's the sustained variety that stands out most: forest, alpine meadow, river valley, and high pass, all within a single continuous circuit around one mountain, which is rarer than it sounds in a route this accessible.

## Would We Do It Again
