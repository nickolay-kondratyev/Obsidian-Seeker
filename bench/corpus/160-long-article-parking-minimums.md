# Eliminating minimum parking requirements

Every zoning code I've pulled apart for this dissertation contains some version of the same buried assumption: that a housing unit generates a fixed, predictable number of car trips, and that the code's job is to guarantee a parking space for each one before a resident moves in. Minimum parking requirements — usually one or two spaces per unit — have been a near-universal feature of American zoning since the 1950s, and they are almost never revisited once written. The argument here is straightforward: minimum parking requirements should be eliminated, not reduced or "modernized" with a lower ratio, but removed entirely, letting the market and site conditions determine how much parking actually gets built.

The case against parking minimums isn't really a case against cars; the framing gets misread constantly in public meetings. It's a case against a poorly justified regulatory mechanism that imposes enormous costs while delivering benefits that were never rigorously established. Adeyemi's 2019 survey of zoning ordinances across forty mid-sized cities found that the vast majority of parking ratios were copied, sometimes verbatim, from a single 1965 planning manual, with no local traffic study, no cost-benefit analysis, and no revision since. That's not evidence-based regulation. That's institutional inertia wearing the costume of technical expertise.

The costs of that inertia are not abstract. A single structured parking space costs tens of thousands of dollars to build, and that cost gets passed directly into rents whether or not a given tenant owns a car. In a housing market already defined by scarcity, forcing developers to build parking nobody asked for functions as a tax on housing production, paid disproportionately by renters who don't drive — a group that skews lower-income, older, and disabled relative to the renter population as a whole. This isn't a fringe position anymore, either. Cities have started acting on it: several jurisdictions in the last five years have eliminated minimums citywide or near transit, and the reform coalition behind these changes spans an unusually wide range of ideological positions, from market urbanists focused on housing supply to environmental groups focused on emissions to disability advocates focused on the fact that parking minimums often crowd out the sidewalk width that actually makes a building accessible.

## Building the argument

The strongest version of this argument doesn't rest on any single data point but on a structural mismatch: zoning codes treat parking demand as a fixed physical fact when it is, in reality, a variable that responds heavily to price, location, and transit access. A code that mandates the same ratio for a downtown apartment three blocks from a rail station and a suburban townhouse with no transit service at all is not responding to demand — it's imposing a single, one-size-fits-all assumption onto radically different contexts and calling the result a technical standard, when it is really closer to a guess frozen in place decades ago and never revisited since, quietly outliving every planner who originally signed off on it.

## Evidence

Look at what happens when the mandate is lifted rather than merely reduced. In jurisdictions that removed minimums entirely, developers did not stop building parking — they built less of it, and they built it in locations and quantities that more closely matched observed demand rather than a decades-old ratio copied from elsewhere. Adeyemi's follow-up study found post-reform buildings averaging parking utilization rates 20 to 30 points higher than pre-reform stock, meaning the parking that does get built is actually used, not sitting empty as sunk cost baked permanently into the site plan. Voss's fieldwork in three transit-adjacent neighborhoods found buildings built to code-minimum ratios sitting at 40 to 60 percent utilization years after opening, which means the land under those empty spaces could have been additional housing units, ground-floor retail, or usable green space, and instead sits as unused concrete no resident ever asked for. Reduced-parking developments in reform cities also leased up measurably faster than comparable code-minimum buildings nearby, likely because lower construction costs translated into somewhat lower asking rents at delivery.

## Counterarguments

The most common objection is spillover: without required parking, residents without off-street spaces will park on neighboring streets, degrading quality of life for existing residents who already feel like they're fighting for a spot on their own block. This is a real concern in specific contexts, particularly low-density neighborhoods with no existing on-street permit system, and it deserves an honest answer rather than dismissal from reform advocates who sometimes wave it away too quickly. But the answer isn't a blanket minimum applied everywhere regardless of context — it's targeted tools like residential permit zones, priced curb management, or context-sensitive design review at the site level, which address the actual externality directly instead of forcing every unit, everywhere, to internalize a worst-case assumption about where its future residents might eventually choose to park their cars. A second objection, that removing minimums will simply hand parking decisions entirely to developers who don't care about neighbors, misunderstands what elimination actually does: it removes a floor, not a ceiling, and neighborhoods can still negotiate site-specific conditions through design review where genuinely warranted. A third objection worth taking seriously is equity for existing car-owning residents in reform neighborhoods who bought or rented under the old assumption of guaranteed nearby parking; transition periods and phased permit-zone rollouts can soften that disruption without abandoning the underlying reform, which is worth doing even if the rollout itself takes a few extra budget cycles to get right.

## Conclusion

Parking minimums persist not because they reflect careful reasoning but because no one has defended them recently. They impose real costs on housing supply and affordability, while their intended benefits can be achieved more precisely through other tools. Cities have already run the experiment.
