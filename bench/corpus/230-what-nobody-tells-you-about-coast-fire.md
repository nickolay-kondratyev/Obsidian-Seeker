# What nobody tells you about coast FIRE

Coast FIRE gets described in most articles as a clean milestone: the moment your existing portfolio, left alone with no further contributions, will grow to your full retirement number by a traditional retirement age through market returns alone. Once you hit it, the story goes, you're free to stop saving for retirement specifically and just cover current living costs from ongoing income, work less, switch to something lower-stress, or simply stop optimizing so hard. What the clean version leaves out is how genuinely strange it feels in practice, because unlike a full FI number, hitting coast FIRE doesn't actually change your day-to-day life unless you make an active decision to change something about it. The portfolio number changes, the spreadsheet cell turns green, and everything else about your Tuesday stays exactly the same, which is a strange kind of anticlimax after years of treating that number as the milestone that mattered most. We hit our own coast number about four months ago, quietly, almost by accident, noticing it during a routine [[221-quarterly-net-worth-checkin]] rather than as a planned celebration, and neither of us has actually changed anything material about our work or spending since, which itself became an interesting question worth sitting with rather than rushing past. Are we choosing to keep working at full pace, or just inertia dressed up as choice because nothing forced the conversation the way a full FI number eventually will? I don't think we've fully answered that yet, and I suspect a lot of people who hit this milestone quietly skip past the question entirely, treating coast FIRE purely as a math checkpoint on the way to something else rather than a genuine decision point worth pausing at deliberately. Priyam described something similar when she hit her own coast number last year: she kept working the exact same hours for another eight months out of pure momentum before she consciously noticed she had permission to slow down and had simply never exercised it, which she now describes as one of the more useful realizations of her whole journey, more useful in some ways than the number itself.

## The math is more sensitive than people expect

The coast number is far more sensitive to your assumed return rate and time horizon than the full FI number is, since you're compounding a fixed amount over decades rather than adding new contributions to smooth out variance. A one-point difference in assumed real return, say 6% versus 7%, can shift your coast number by tens of thousands of dollars for a horizon twenty-plus years out, which means the milestone is fuzzier than the clean headline number implies, more a range than a precise line you cross on a specific day. Inflation assumptions matter just as much and get discussed less: most calculators bake in a specific long-run inflation rate, and changing that single hidden assumption moves the coast number by a similar order of magnitude as the return-rate sensitivity above.

## It doesn't answer the actual question

Hitting coast FIRE tells you that you technically could stop saving for retirement without derailing the eventual outcome, but it says nothing about whether you should, whether your current job still serves you, or whether "coasting" even looks like anything different from what you're already doing day to day. That's a separate, harder question the math alone can't answer, and treating the milestone as self-explanatory skips past exactly the part that actually matters for how you spend your remaining working years. It's a bit like calculating that you technically have enough calories banked to skip a meal: true, and possibly useful, but it doesn't tell you whether skipping that particular meal is actually a good idea given everything else going on that day, and the number itself has no opinion on the question either way, however precisely it's been calculated, and however confidently the spreadsheet presents it as a settled fact rather than a starting point for a much messier conversation about time and purpose.

## What we're doing about it

Nothing dramatic yet, honestly, but we've started talking more seriously about what "coasting" would concretely look like for each of us individually rather than as an abstract couple-level concept, since our jobs and tolerances for change are different enough that a shared answer probably doesn't exist. Dana leans toward reducing hours at her current job if that's even an option worth asking about formally; I lean toward staying full-time a while longer but being pickier about what projects I take on, since money pressure is no longer the forcing function it used to be for saying yes to things I don't actually want to do. We've also started keeping a running note of what an ideal reduced-hours week would even look like for each of us, concretely, down to which specific days would change and what we'd actually do with the freed-up time, since neither of us wants "coasting" to just mean more unstructured scrolling instead of something we've genuinely thought through. Priyam suggested we each write a short, honest answer independently before comparing notes, specifically to avoid one of us quietly deferring to the other's louder preference the way couples sometimes do without noticing it happening, and we're planning to actually do that exercise before the next quarterly check-in rather than let it slide the way half-planned exercises like this usually do around here, buried under whatever more urgent thing shows up that particular week and quietly never resurfaces again until months later when someone remembers it existed.

## Where this leaves us

Not urgent, but not nothing either. We're giving ourselves through the next annual review to actually decide whether coast FIRE changes anything concrete, rather than let the milestone quietly become just another number that passed by unremarked.
