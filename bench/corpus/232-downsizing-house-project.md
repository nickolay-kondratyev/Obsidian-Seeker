# Downsizing house project

We've circled this idea for over a year — see mentions in [[211-budget-review-sync]] and [[231-money-date-check-in]] — and it's finally getting a proper doc instead of staying a vague someday conversation over coffee. The core question: does selling our current place and buying something smaller meaningfully accelerate the early-retirement timeline, or is it mostly emotional decluttering dressed up as financial strategy. Both could be true at once, honestly, and that's fine as long as we're clear-eyed about which motivation is driving which decision going forward.

## Current situation and motivation

Four bedrooms, two of which we haven't meaningfully used in three years since Dana's parents stopped visiting for long stretches. Mortgage balance is roughly $210k against an estimated value near $480k, giving us significant equity that's currently doing nothing but sitting there, untouched, while smaller-footprint options exist nearby at lower price points with lower carrying costs across taxes, utilities, and maintenance combined. Lower ongoing costs is the obvious driver — smaller places mean smaller property tax bills, smaller utility bills, and less deferred maintenance piling up in the background that we've been quietly ignoring for longer than we should admit. Beyond the math, though, there's something appealing about actively shaping the space around the life we're moving toward rather than maintaining a house sized for a version of our future we're no longer sure we want.

## The math, rough first pass

Selling at $480k minus roughly 6% in closing and agent costs nets about $451k. Paying off the $210k mortgage leaves around $241k in freed equity, which is a meaningful chunk to either invest directly or apply toward a smaller place with a much smaller loan attached to it, or none at all depending on rates that year. A smaller place at $320k, financed conservatively or paid mostly in cash, changes our monthly housing cost meaningfully. Rough estimate: current housing costs run about $2,400 a month all-in, including taxes, insurance, utilities, and an averaged maintenance reserve; a smaller paid-down place could bring that closer to $1,100 a month, a difference that compounds hard over a decade if invested rather than spent, easily worth six figures by the time we'd actually retire. Even accounting for a conservative return assumption, that gap alone could shave a meaningful chunk off our target date, which is the whole reason this is worth modeling properly instead of dismissing it as just a nice-to-have.

## Risks and open questions

Moving costs, renovation surprises in an older smaller home, and the emotional cost of leaving a house we've made real memories in are all real considerations, not just numbers on a page. Also unclear how a move would affect proximity to the [[226-fire-meetup-quarterly-checkin]] group and our current support network, and whether a smaller home would even suit us long-term.

## Next steps

Tour a few smaller listings this spring without committing to anything real yet, and get a proper appraisal on our current place so the equity estimate above stops being a guess pulled from a real-estate app's automated valuation. Talk to a lender about what a smaller mortgage, or no mortgage at all, would actually look like given current rates, since that changes the monthly-savings math meaningfully either way. Ask Priyam, who went through something similar with a smaller-scale move two years back, what she wishes she'd checked before listing her old place. Bring firmer numbers to the next [[216-annual-financial-planning-meeting]] cycle before deciding anything real, rather than letting this stay an open-ended conversation indefinitely the way it has been for the past year.
