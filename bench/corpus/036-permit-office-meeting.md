Meeting at the county permit office, in person rather than by phone, since the reviewer flagged something in our kitchen and workshop applications that she wanted to walk through with a site plan in hand. Present: homeowner, general contractor Kofi, and the permit reviewer, whose name badge just said "Reviewer" so I never actually caught it. This was scheduled after two weeks of back-and-forth over email that wasn't resolving anything, and Kofi suggested an in-person visit usually moves things faster than another round of emailed clarifications, which turned out to be right. We brought the full site plan, the structural sketch for the header beam, and photos of the existing kitchen and workshop conditions, since the reviewer had specifically asked for "as-built" documentation before our first submission even went in.

## Kitchen Permit Discussion

The reviewer's main question was about the header beam replacing the load-bearing wall between kitchen and dining room; she wanted confirmation from a licensed structural engineer rather than just the contractor's sketch, which we hadn't budgeted for but apparently is standard for any wall removal regardless of scope, even when the wall isn't going away entirely. Kofi pushed back gently, explaining that the wall itself isn't being removed, just a portion opened up with a header, and asked whether a simplified engineering letter rather than full stamped drawings would satisfy the requirement, given the modest span involved. She agreed a letter would work, provided it came from an engineer licensed in the state and included calculated beam sizing rather than just a general sign-off, which is apparently a distinction that trips up a lot of applicants who assume any contractor sign-off is interchangeable with an engineer's stamp. We're now waiting on a referral from Kofi's usual engineer, who he thinks can turn the letter around within a week given how straightforward the span actually is compared to some of the full room additions he normally reviews. The reviewer also mentioned that once the letter is filed, the kitchen application shouldn't need another in-person visit, which was reassuring given how much of the morning this one took out of both our schedules. She did note that the letter needs to specifically reference the beam's bearing points on both ends, not just the span, since a previous applicant's letter had been kicked back for omitting that detail and she didn't want us to hit the same delay a second time around.

## Workshop Permit Discussion

The workshop application raised more questions than the kitchen one, mostly because it involves new electrical service to a detached structure, which triggers a different review track than interior renovation work. The reviewer wanted clarification on amperage: our application listed a 100-amp subpanel, and she asked whether that was based on an actual load calculation or just a round number Kofi's electrician picked, since underestimating amperage is a common reason workshop permits get sent back for revision later in the process. It was, in fact, closer to a round number, so we're now getting an actual load calc done before resubmitting, factoring in the table saw, dust collector, welder, and space heater we're planning to run out there, since all four together draw more than a homeowner might expect from what looks like a simple shed on paper. She also flagged that the distance from the workshop to the property line is close enough to the setback minimum that she wants a stamped survey rather than our contractor's tape-measure numbers, which means hiring a surveyor, an unplanned cost that's going into the [[052-budget-tracking-overview]] under contingency rather than the workshop's own line item, since it wasn't anticipated at all when we drew up that budget originally. Kofi thinks the survey can happen within two weeks, and estimated the cost around four hundred dollars, though he was clear that's a rough guess and not a quote from an actual surveyor yet. She also asked, almost as an aside, whether the workshop had a wood stove or any other combustion source, since that changes the fire separation requirements from the house; it doesn't, just the space heater already mentioned, which she said keeps us on the simpler track for that particular question at least, one small mercy in an otherwise longer list of open items than we'd hoped to leave with today. Kofi asked, mostly for planning purposes, whether the workshop review would run in parallel with the kitchen once both were resubmitted, and she confirmed it would, since they're on different reviewer tracks internally despite her personally handling both for us as a courtesy.

## Decisions

We agreed to a phased resubmission: kitchen documents first, since they only need the engineering letter, targeting resubmission by the end of next week, followed by workshop documents once the load calculation and survey are both in hand. The reviewer confirmed that phasing the two submissions won't slow down the kitchen review, since they're tracked as genuinely separate applications in the county's system despite both belonging to the same property address and the same overall renovation project. She also confirmed that once resubmitted with complete documentation, turnaround should be back to the standard ten to fourteen business days rather than sitting in the open-question queue where incomplete applications apparently wait indefinitely with no proactive follow-up from her office. Kofi asked whether we could request the same reviewer for the resubmission, mostly so we wouldn't have to re-explain the header beam situation from scratch to someone new, and she said she'd flag both files under her own queue, which felt like a genuinely useful thing to have secured before we left. We left with a much clearer sense of what's actually blocking approval than the email chain had given us in two full weeks, and Kofi said he'd rather do one more in-person visit like this than another month of emails that go nowhere, a sentiment I fully share after today. The reviewer gave us her direct line for follow-up questions, which apparently isn't standard practice, so we're treating that as a small win worth protecting by not overusing it.
